4.1 Requirements, readers and stakeholders Must know

Before an architect draws a house, the owner says what is needed: "three bedrooms, a kitchen facing east, a total cost under 40 lakh rupees, and it must follow the city building code". The first two are services the house must provide; the last two are constraints. Software is the same. The list of services and constraints is the requirements; the work of finding, writing and checking that list is requirements engineering.

The requirements for a system are the descriptions of the services that a system should provide and the constraints on its operation. They reflect what customers need from a system that serves a purpose such as controlling a device, placing an order or finding information.

Requirements engineering (RE) — the process of establishing the services that a customer requires from a system and the constraints under which it operates and is developed.

System requirements — the descriptions of the system services and constraints that are generated during the requirements engineering process.

Sommerville's wording is also worth quoting: the process of finding out, analysing, documenting and checking these services and constraints is called requirements engineering.

What is a requirement? Important

The term is not used consistently in industry. A requirement may range from a high-level abstract statement of a service or of a system constraint to a detailed mathematical functional specification. This is inevitable because requirements may serve a dual function:

  • They may be the basis for a bid for a contract — therefore they must be open to interpretation, so that different companies can propose different solutions.
  • They may be the basis for the contract itself — therefore they must be defined in detail.
  • Both these statements may be called "requirements".

"If a company wishes to let a contract for a large software development project, it must define its needs in a sufficiently abstract way that a solution is not pre-defined. The requirements must be written so that several contractors can bid for the contract, offering, perhaps, different ways of meeting the client organization's needs. Once a contract has been awarded, the contractor must write a system definition for the client in more detail so that the client understands and can validate what the software will do. Both of these documents may be called the requirements document for the system."

In short: an abstract version is written so that contractors can bid; a detailed version is written after the contract is awarded so that the client can check (validate) what will be built. Many problems in RE come from not separating these two levels clearly — which is why we name them separately.

User requirements and system requirements Must know

User requirements — statements in natural language plus diagrams of the services the system provides and its operational constraints. Written for customers.

System requirements — a structured document setting out detailed descriptions of the system's functions, services and operational constraints. It defines what should be implemented, so it may be part of a contract between client and contractor.

Requirements are written at different levels of detail because different readers use them in different ways (Fig 4.2). System stakeholders Must know

The readers in Fig 4.2 are examples of stakeholders. Besides users, many other people have an interest in a system.

System stakeholder — any person or organization who is affected by the system in some way and so who has a legitimate interest in it.

Stakeholder types (range from end-users through managers to external stakeholders):

  • End users — the people who use the system in their daily work.
  • System managers — the people who manage and run the system.
  • System owners — the organisation that pays for and owns the system.
  • External stakeholders — e.g. regulators, who certify the acceptability of the system.

Before RE starts: the feasibility study Read once

Some understanding of the requirements is needed before deciding to build or buy a system at all. This early RE gives a high-level view of what the system might do and its benefits, which is assessed in a feasibility study — a short, focused study early in the RE process that answers three questions: (1) Does the system contribute to the overall objectives of the organisation? (2) Can it be implemented within schedule and budget using current technology? (3) Can it be integrated with other systems that are used? If any answer is "no", you should probably not go ahead.

Agile methods and requirements Important

  • Many agile methods argue that producing detailed system requirements is a waste of time, as requirements change so quickly.
  • The requirements document is therefore always out of date.
  • Agile methods usually use incremental requirements engineering and may express requirements as user stories (Chapter 3), written on cards or whiteboards; the user prioritises the stories for the next increment.
  • This is practical for business systems but problematic for systems that require pre-delivery analysis (e.g. critical systems) or systems developed by several teams.

Sommerville's middle path: even with agile development, write a short supporting document that defines the business and dependability requirements, because requirements that apply to the whole system are easily forgotten when you focus on the features of the next release.

4.2 Functional and non-functional requirements Must know

Functional = what the system does (search, generate a report, identify a user). Non-functional = how well it must do it and under which rules (how fast, how reliable, how secure, which laws and standards apply). For a car, "the car shall have brakes" is functional; "it shall stop from 100 km/h within 40 metres" and "it shall meet emission laws" are non-functional.

Functional requirements — statements of services the system should provide, how the system should react to particular inputs and how the system should behave in particular situations. They may also state what the system should not do.

Non-functional requirements — constraints on the services or functions offered by the system, such as timing constraints, constraints on the development process, standards, etc. They often apply to the system as a whole rather than to individual features or services.

Domain requirements — constraints on the system from the domain of operation (the application area, e.g. medicine, banking, railways).

The line between them is not sharp. A user requirement about security, such as limiting access to authorised users, looks non-functional. When developed in more detail it generates requirements that are clearly functional, such as including user authentication facilities. Requirements are not independent: one requirement often generates or constrains other requirements (Fig 4.4).

Functional requirements Must know

  • Describe functionality or system services — what the system should do. They are statements of the services the system must provide, or descriptions of how some computations must be carried out.
  • Depend on the type of software, the expected users and the type of system where the software is used.
  • Functional user requirements may be high-level statements of what the system should do. They are written in natural language so that users and managers can understand them.
  • Functional system requirements should describe the system services in detail — the functions, their inputs and outputs, and exceptions. They are written for developers.
  1. A user shall be able to search the appointments lists for all clinics.
  2. The system shall generate each day, for each clinic, a list of patients who are expected to attend appointments that day.
  3. Each staff member using the system shall be uniquely identified by his or her 8-digit employee number.

Functional requirements can be written at different levels of detail: requirement 1 is general, requirement 3 is very specific.

Information requirements: if an off-the-shelf product will meet the need, a detailed functional specification is pointless. Focus instead on information requirements — what information people need to do their work and how it is delivered and organised (e.g. what goes into the daily list of expected patients).

Domain requirements Important

Domain requirements are derived from the application domain of the system rather than from the specific needs of system users. They may:

  • be new functional requirements in their own right,
  • constrain existing functional requirements, or
  • set out how particular computations must be carried out.

The problem with domain requirements: software engineers may not understand the characteristics of the domain, so they may not know whether a domain requirement has been missed out or conflicts with other requirements. Domain requirements are found during requirements discovery, from stakeholders and from documentation (Section 4.4). For instance, the insulin pump's rule for computing a dose from the rate of change of blood sugar (Section 4.5) comes from medical knowledge, not from a user's personal wish — it sets out how a computation must be carried out.

Requirements imprecision Must know

  • Problems arise when functional requirements are not precisely stated.
  • Ambiguous requirements may be interpreted in different ways by developers and users.
  • A developer naturally chooses the interpretation that is simplest to implement. If that is not what the customer wanted, new requirements and changes are needed — causing disputes, delayed delivery and higher cost.

Consider the term "search" in Mentcare requirement 1:

User intention

Search for a patient name across all appointments in all clinics. Rationale: patients with mental health problems are sometimes confused and may go to a different clinic from the one booked.

Developer interpretation

Search for a patient name in an individual clinic: the user chooses a clinic, then searches. Easier to build, but needs more user input and takes longer.

Requirements completeness and consistency Must know

In principle, requirements should be both complete and consistent:

Complete
They should include descriptions of all facilities (services and information) required.
Consistent
There should be no conflicts or contradictions in the descriptions of the system facilities.

In practice, because of system and environmental complexity, it is impossible to produce a complete and consistent requirements document (except for very small systems). Two reasons: (1) it is easy to make mistakes and omissions when writing specifications for large, complex systems; (2) large systems have many stakeholders with different backgrounds and often inconsistent needs, and the inconsistencies may only appear after deeper analysis or during development.

Non-functional requirements Must know

  • NFRs define system properties and constraints, e.g. reliability, response time and storage requirements. Constraints are I/O device capability, system representations (the data representations used in interfaces), etc.
  • Process requirements may also be specified, mandating a particular IDE, programming language or development method.
  • NFRs often relate to emergent properties — properties of the system as a whole (such as reliability, response time, memory use) rather than of one component.
  • NFRs may be more critical than functional requirements. If these are not met, the system may be useless.

Users can usually find a way round a function that does not quite meet their needs, but not round a failed NFR. If an aircraft system does not meet its reliability requirements, it will not be certified as safe for operation; if an embedded control system fails to meet its performance requirements, the control functions will not operate correctly.

Non-functional requirements implementation

You can usually point to the component that implements a functional requirement (e.g. a formatting component for reports). NFRs are harder to locate — their implementation may be spread throughout the system, for two reasons:

  1. NFRs may affect the overall architecture of a system rather than the individual components. For example, to ensure that performance requirements are met, you may have to organise the system to minimise communications between components.
  2. A single NFR, such as a security requirement, may generate a number of related functional requirements that define system services that are required. It may also generate requirements that restrict existing requirements (Fig 4.4).

Types of non-functional requirement Must know

NFRs arise from user needs because of budget constraints, organisational policies, the need for interoperability with other software or hardware systems, or external factors such as safety regulations or privacy legislation. Sommerville classifies them by where they come from: the product, the organisation, or outside (Fig 4.5).

Goals and verifiable requirements Must know

Non-functional requirements may be very difficult to state precisely, and imprecise requirements may be difficult to verify. Stakeholders often propose NFRs as general goals — ease of use, the ability to recover from failure, rapid user response. Goals set out good intentions but leave room for interpretation and for dispute once the system is delivered.

Goal — a general intention of the user, such as ease of use.

Verifiable non-functional requirement — a statement using some measure that can be objectively tested.

Goals are still helpful to developers, as they convey the intentions of the system users. Whenever possible, though, write NFRs quantitatively so they can be objectively tested.

Usability requirementStatement
Goal (not testable)"The system should be easy to use by medical staff and should be organized in such a way that user errors are minimized."
Testable NFR"Medical staff shall be able to use all the system functions after four hours of training. After this training, the average number of errors made by experienced users shall not exceed two per hour of system use."

Your slides say four hours of training; the 10th-edition textbook text says two hours. Either is accepted — what matters is that the rewritten requirement has measurable numbers (training time, errors per hour). These can be checked by software instrumentation that counts user errors during testing.

Why quantifying NFRs is hard: customers find it difficult to translate goals into measurable requirements; for some goals, such as maintainability, there are no simple metrics; customers may not relate a number (e.g. for reliability) to their everyday experience; and objectively verifying measurable NFRs can be very expensive.

NFR conflicts and where to put NFRs

  • NFRs often conflict and interact with other requirements. The Mentcare ID-card requirement needs a card reader on each computer, but another requirement may ask for mobile access from doctors' and nurses' tablets or smartphones, which have no card readers — so an alternative identification method may be needed.
  • It is difficult to separate FRs and NFRs in the requirements document. If NFRs are stated separately, relationships are hard to see; ideally, highlight NFRs related to emergent properties (performance, reliability) in a separate section or by marking them.
  • NFRs such as reliability, safety and confidentiality are particularly important for critical systems.

A verifiable NFR = property + measure + number + unit + condition. For example: "The system shall display the results of a patient-name search within 2 seconds for 95% of searches when 50 users are logged in." Examiners reward the number.

4.3 Requirements engineering processes Must know

The processes used for RE vary widely depending on the application domain, the people involved and the organisation developing the requirements. However, there are a number of generic activities common to all processes:

  1. Requirements elicitation — discovering requirements by working with stakeholders.
  2. Requirements analysis — classifying, organising, prioritising and negotiating them (done together with elicitation).
  3. Requirements validation — checking that the requirements define the system the customer really wants.
  4. Requirements management — managing changes to the requirements.

The textbook describes the same work as three key activities: discovering requirements by interacting with stakeholders (elicitation and analysis); converting them into a standard form (specification); and checking that they define the system the customer wants (validation) — with requirements management handling change. In practice, RE is an iterative activity in which these processes are interleaved.

Slides: 4 generic activities (elicitation, analysis, validation, management). Textbook: 3 key activities (elicitation & analysis, specification, validation). In the exam write elicitation & analysis, specification, validation and management — that covers both versions.

A spiral view of the requirements engineering process Must know

  • The three activities — elicitation, specification and validation — are organised as an iterative process around a spiral. Each loop adds detail: business → user → system requirements.
  • The output of the RE process is a system requirements document.
  • The time and effort spent on each activity in an iteration depends on the stage of the overall process, the type of system being developed and the budget available.
  • Early in the process, most effort goes on understanding high-level business and non-functional requirements and the user requirements. Later, in the outer rings, more effort goes on eliciting and understanding the NFRs and more detailed system requirements.
  • The number of iterations can vary: the spiral can be exited after some or all of the user requirements have been elicited. Agile development can be used instead of prototyping, so that the requirements and the implementation are developed together.
  • In virtually all systems, requirements change (people understand their needs better, the buying organisation changes, the hardware, software and organisational environment changes). Changes must be managed — see Section 4.7.

To draw Fig 4.7 quickly: draw a "Y" (three lines from a centre); label the sectors specification (top), elicitation (left), validation (right); draw a spiral outwards from the centre; write the nine activities along it in order — Start → business requirements spec → feasibility study → user requirements elicitation → user requirements spec → prototyping → system requirements elicitation → system requirements spec & modelling → reviews — and end with an arrow to "System requirements document".

4.4 Requirements elicitation Must know

"Elicit" means to draw something out. Requirements rarely arrive written down — you draw them out of people by talking to them, watching them work and discussing realistic examples with them.

Requirements elicitation and analysis (sometimes called requirements elicitation or requirements discovery):

  • Involves technical staff working with customers to find out about the application domain, the services that the system should provide and the system's operational constraints.
  • May involve end-users, managers, engineers involved in maintenance, domain experts, trade unions, etc. These are called stakeholders.

The aim is to understand the work that stakeholders do and how they might use a new system to support it. Software engineers work with a range of system stakeholders to find out about the application domain, work activities, the services and features wanted, the required system performance, hardware constraints, other systems, etc. The stages are: requirements discovery; requirements classification and organisation; requirements prioritisation and negotiation; and requirements specification.

Problems of requirements elicitation Must know

The requirements elicitation and analysis process Must know

  1. Requirements discovery — interacting with stakeholders to discover their requirements. Domain requirements (from stakeholders and documentation) are also discovered at this stage.
  2. Requirements classification and organisation — takes the unstructured collection of requirements, groups related requirements and organises them into coherent clusters.
  3. Prioritisation and negotiation — prioritising requirements and resolving requirements conflicts. With many stakeholders, requirements will conflict; stakeholders usually meet to agree compromise requirements.
  4. Requirements specification — requirements are documented and input into the next round of the spiral. An early draft of the requirements document may be produced, or requirements kept informally on whiteboards, wikis or other shared spaces.
  • The process is iterative, with continual feedback between activities. It starts with discovery and ends with documentation; the analyst's understanding improves each round; it ends when the requirements document is produced.
  • Each organisation has its own version of this general model, depending on local factors: staff expertise, the type of system and the standards used.
  • Viewpoints help organise stakeholder information: a viewpoint collects the requirements of one group of stakeholders who have something in common (e.g. end-users, managers). Viewpoints can also represent domain requirements and constraints from other systems. Alternatively, a model of the system architecture can be used to link requirements to subsystems.
  • Hold regular stakeholder meetings: stakeholders who feel their views were ignored may deliberately try to undermine the RE process.
  • When documenting, use simple language and diagrams, and a shared document or wiki that all stakeholders can access.

Requirements discovery Must know

Requirements discovery — the process of gathering information about the required and existing systems and distilling the user and system requirements from this information.

  • Interaction is with system stakeholders, from managers to external regulators.
  • Systems normally have a range of stakeholders.
  • Stakeholder information is supplemented by knowledge of existing systems and their usage, and by documents of various kinds.

There are two fundamental approaches: interviewing, where you talk to people about what they do, and observation or ethnography, where you watch people doing their job to see what artifacts they use and how they use them. Use a mix of both; stories and scenarios then help to discuss the requirements you derive.

Interviewing Must know

Formal or informal interviews with stakeholders are part of most RE processes. The RE team asks stakeholders about the system they use now and the system to be developed, and derives requirements from the answers.

Closed interviews

Based on a pre-determined list of questions: the stakeholder answers a predefined set of questions.

Open interviews

No predefined agenda: various issues are explored with stakeholders to develop a better understanding of their needs.

Effective interviewing

  • Be open-minded, avoid pre-conceived ideas about the requirements, and be willing to listen to stakeholders — if a stakeholder suggests something surprising, be willing to change your mind about the system.
  • Prompt the interviewee to get discussions going using a springboard question, a requirements proposal, or by working together on a prototype system. Saying "tell me what you want" rarely gives useful information; people talk more easily in a defined context.

Interviews in practice

  • Normally a mix of closed and open-ended interviewing: some questions must be answered, and these lead to less structured discussion. Completely open-ended discussions rarely work well.
  • Interviews are good for getting an overall understanding of what stakeholders do, how they might interact with the system, and the difficulties they face with current systems.
  • Interviewers need to be open-minded, without pre-conceived ideas of what the system should do.
  • You need to prompt the user to talk about the system by suggesting requirements rather than simply asking them what they want. Unless you have a prototype to show, don't expect specific, detailed requirements — you analyse the information and generate the requirements yourself.

Problems with interviews

  • Application specialists may use language (jargon) to describe their work that isn't easy for the requirements engineer to understand.
  • Interviews are not good for understanding domain requirements:
    • requirements engineers cannot understand specific domain terminology;
    • some domain knowledge is so familiar that people find it hard to articulate, or think it isn't worth articulating. (For a librarian, it goes without saying that all acquisitions are catalogued before being added to the library — so the interviewer never hears it.)
  • Interviews are also poor for organisational requirements and constraints: there are subtle power relationships, and people are reluctant to reveal the real (not the published) structure of decision-making to a stranger.
  • On its own, interviewing is liable to miss essential information, so use it together with other techniques.

Ethnography Must know

Software systems are used in a social and organisational environment. One reason why many systems are delivered but never used is that their requirements do not take proper account of how social and organisational factors affect the practical operation of the system.

Ethnography — an observational technique in which a social scientist spends a considerable time observing and analysing how people actually work, immersed in the working environment, to derive requirements for software that supports that work.

  • People do not have to explain or articulate their work — which is useful, because their work is often "second nature" to them.
  • Social and organisational factors of importance may be observed. (E.g. a workgroup may self-organise so that members can cover for each other — something never mentioned in an interview.)
  • Ethnographic studies have shown that work is usually richer and more complex than suggested by simple system models. Suchman's pioneering study of office work found real practice far richer than office-automation models assumed — the main reason those systems had no significant effect on productivity.
  • Its value is that it discovers implicit requirements that reflect how people actually work, not the formal processes defined by the organisation.

Scope of ethnography

Ethnography is particularly effective for discovering two types of requirements:

  1. Requirements derived from the way that people actually work, rather than the way in which process definitions suggest that they ought to work. Example: air traffic controllers may switch off a conflict-alert system that sounds warnings even when planes are far apart, and use other strategies to keep planes on safe paths.
  2. Requirements derived from cooperation and awareness of other people's activities — awareness of what other people are doing leads to changes in the ways in which we do things. Example: controllers watch adjacent sectors to predict how many aircraft will enter their own sector, so an automated ATC system should show some of the work in adjacent sectors.

Limitation: ethnography is effective for understanding existing processes but cannot identify new features that should be added to a system. Commentators suggest Nokia used ethnography to study how people used their phones, while Apple ignored current use and revolutionised the industry with the iPhone. Because it focuses on the end-user, ethnography is also weak for broader organisational or domain requirements — so it is one technique among several.

Focused ethnography

  • Developed in a project studying the air traffic control process.
  • Combines ethnography with prototyping.
  • Prototype development results in unanswered questions, which focus the ethnographic analysis.
  • The problem with ethnography is that it studies existing practices, which may have some historical basis that is no longer relevant.

The ethnography informs the development of the prototype, so fewer prototype refinement cycles are needed. The prototyping focuses the ethnography by identifying problems and questions, which are discussed with the ethnographer in debriefing meetings; the ethnographer then looks for answers in the next phase of the study.

Stories and scenarios Must know

  • Scenarios and user stories are real-life examples of how a system can be used.
  • Stories and scenarios are a description of how a system may be used for a particular task — what people do, what information they use and produce, and what systems they may use.
  • Because they are based on a practical situation, stakeholders can relate to them and can comment on their situation with respect to the story.

Story vs scenario: both describe system use for a task; they differ in structure and level of detail. A story is narrative text giving a high-level, "big picture" description. A scenario is a structured form of user story with specific information such as inputs and outputs. Parts of a story are developed into scenarios.

Jack is a primary school teacher in Ullapool (a village in northern Scotland). He has decided that a class project should be focused around the fishing industry in the area, looking at the history, development and economic impact of fishing. As part of this, pupils are asked to gather and share reminiscences from relatives, use newspaper archives and collect old photographs related to fishing and fishing communities in the area. Pupils use an iLearn wiki to gather together fishing stories and SCRAN (a history resources site) to access newspaper archives and photographs. However, Jack also needs a photo sharing site as he wants pupils to take and comment on each other's photos and to upload scans of old photographs that they may have in their families.

Jack sends an email to a primary school teachers group, which he is a member of, to see if anyone can recommend an appropriate system. Two teachers reply and both suggest that he uses KidsTakePics, a photo sharing site that allows teachers to check and moderate content. As KidsTakePics is not integrated with the iLearn authentication service, he sets up a teacher and a class account. He uses the iLearn setup service to add KidsTakePics to the services seen by the pupils in his class so that when they log in, they can immediately use the system to upload photos from their mobile devices and class computers.

The advantage of stories is that everyone can relate to them. The iLearn team put such stories on a wiki and invited teachers and students across the country to comment — reaching a wider community than they could ever interview. User stories in agile methods such as Extreme Programming are really narrative scenarios.

What a scenario should include

A scenario is a structured form of user story. It starts with an outline of the interaction, and details are added during elicitation. Scenarios should include:

  1. A description of the starting situation (what the system and users expect when the scenario starts);
  2. a description of the normal flow of events;
  3. a description of what can go wrong (and how the resulting problems are handled);
  4. information about other concurrent activities;
  5. a description of the state when the scenario finishes.

S-N-W-O-E — "Start, Normal, Wrong, Others, End". Think of a short film: opening scene, normal plot, what goes wrong, side plots, final scene.

4.5 Requirements specification Must know

Requirements specification — the process of writing down the user and system requirements in a requirements document.

  • User requirements have to be understandable by end-users and customers who do not have a technical background. Write them in natural language with simple tables, forms and intuitive diagrams; avoid software jargon and structured or formal notations; describe only external behaviour, not architecture or design.
  • System requirements are more detailed requirements and may include more technical information. They are the starting point for system design.
  • The requirements may be part of a contract for the system development, so it is important that they are as complete as possible.
  • Ideally requirements are clear, unambiguous, easy to understand, complete and consistent. In practice this is almost impossible, because stakeholders interpret requirements differently and there are inherent conflicts.

Ways of writing a system requirements specification Must know

NotationDescription
Natural languageThe requirements are written using numbered sentences in natural language. Each sentence should express one requirement.
Structured natural languageThe requirements are written in natural language on a standard form or template. Each field provides information about an aspect of the requirement.
Design description languagesThis approach uses a language like a programming language, but with more abstract features, to specify the requirements by defining an operational model of the system. This approach is now rarely used although it can be useful for interface specifications.
Graphical notationsGraphical models, supplemented by text annotations, are used to define the functional requirements for the system; UML use case and sequence diagrams are commonly used.
Mathematical specificationsThese notations are based on mathematical concepts such as finite-state machines or sets. Although these unambiguous specifications can reduce the ambiguity in a requirements document, most customers don't understand a formal specification. They cannot check that it represents what they want and are reluctant to accept it as a system contract.

N-S-D-G-M — "Nice Students Draw Good Maps": Natural language, Structured natural language, Design description languages, Graphical notations, Mathematical specifications.

Requirements and design Important

In principle, requirements should state what the system should do, and the design should describe how it does this. In practice, requirements and design are inseparable:

  • A system architecture may be designed to structure the requirements — the requirements are organised by subsystem (as was done for iLearn).
  • The system may inter-operate with other systems that generate design requirements (existing systems constrain the design).
  • The use of a specific architecture to satisfy non-functional requirements may be a domain requirement (e.g. N-version programming to achieve reliability).
  • This may be the consequence of a regulatory requirement — a regulator who must certify that the system is safe may insist on an architecture that has already been certified.

Natural language specification Must know

  • Requirements are written as natural language sentences supplemented by diagrams and tables.
  • Natural language is used because it is expressive, intuitive and universal, so the requirements can be understood by users and customers. It has been used since the 1950s and is still the most widely used way of specifying requirements.
  • But it is also potentially vague and ambiguous, and its meaning depends on the background of the reader.

Guidelines for writing requirements

  1. Invent a standard format and use it for all requirements. Standardising makes omissions less likely and requirements easier to check (ideally one or two sentences per requirement).
  2. Use language in a consistent way. Use shall for mandatory requirements and should for desirable requirements.
  3. Use text highlighting (bold, italic or colour) to identify key parts of the requirement.
  4. Avoid the use of computer jargon, abbreviations and acronyms — words like "architecture" and "module" are easily misunderstood.
  5. Include an explanation (rationale) of why a requirement is necessary, and who proposed it (its source), so you know whom to consult if it has to change.

Problems with natural language

ProblemMeaning
Lack of clarityPrecision is difficult without making the document difficult to read.

3.2 The system shall measure the blood sugar and deliver insulin, if required, every 10 minutes. (Changes in blood sugar are relatively slow so more frequent measurement is unnecessary; less frequent measurement could lead to unnecessarily high sugar levels.)

Guidelines in action: a numbered standard format, "shall" for mandatory, the key word highlighted, and the rationale in brackets.

Structured specifications Must know

Structured specification — an approach to writing requirements where the freedom of the requirements writer is limited and requirements are written in a standard way (using templates/forms).

  • Keeps most of the expressiveness and understandability of natural language, but imposes uniformity.
  • This works well for some types of requirements, e.g. requirements for an embedded control system, but is sometimes too rigid for writing business system requirements.
  • Templates may use programming-language constructs to show alternatives and iteration, and highlight key elements with shading or different fonts. The specification can be organised around the objects, functions or events of the system.
  • (In the VOLERE method, each user requirement is first written on a card with fields such as rationale, dependencies on other requirements, source and supporting materials.)

Form-based specifications — the information on the form

  1. Definition of the function or entity.
  2. Description of inputs and where they come from.
  3. Description of outputs and where they go to.
  4. Information about the information needed for the computation and other entities used (the "requires" part).
  5. Description of the action to be taken.
  6. Pre and post conditions (if appropriate) — what must be true before the function is called, and what is true after it.
  7. The side effects (if any) of the function.

Use cases Must know

  • Use cases are a kind of scenario that are included in the UML (they were first introduced in the Objectory method).
  • Use cases identify the actors in an interaction and describe the interaction itself.
  • A set of use cases should describe all possible interactions with the system.
  • A high-level graphical model is supplemented by a more detailed tabular description (Chapter 5).
  • UML sequence diagrams may be used to add detail to use cases by showing the sequence of event processing in the system.

Notation: actors — people or other systems — are drawn as stick figures; each class of interaction is a named ellipse; lines link actors with the interactions they take part in. Arrowheads may be added to show how an interaction is initiated.

Setup consultation allows two or more doctors, working in different offices, to view the same patient record at the same time. One doctor initiates the consultation by choosing the people involved from a dropdown menu of doctors who are online. The patient record is then displayed on their screens, but only the initiating doctor can edit the record. In addition, a text chat window is created to help coordinate actions. It is assumed that a phone call for voice communication can be separately arranged.

Sommerville's view: stakeholders often don't understand the term "use case", don't find the diagram useful and are not interested in every detailed interaction — so use cases are often more helpful in system design than in RE. Some people treat each use case as a single low-level scenario; others (Stevens and Pooley) treat a use case as a set of related scenarios — one for the normal interaction plus one for each possible exception.

The software requirements document Must know

Software requirements document (also called the software requirements specification, SRS) — the official statement of what is required of the system developers. It should include both a definition of user requirements and a specification of the system requirements.

  • It is an agreed statement of the system requirements, organised so that both system customers and software developers can use it.
  • It is NOT a design document. As far as possible, it should set out WHAT the system should do rather than HOW it should do it.
  • It is essential when systems are outsourced, when different teams develop different parts of the system, and when a detailed analysis of the requirements is mandatory. It may not be needed for software products or business systems.
  • Agile methods argue that it is out of date as soon as it is written; they collect user requirements incrementally as short user stories.

Because its users are so diverse, the document is a compromise: it must describe requirements for customers, define them in precise detail for developers and testers, and include information about likely future evolution (which helps designers avoid restrictive decisions and helps maintenance engineers adapt the system).

Requirements document variability

  • Information in the requirements document depends on the type of system and the approach to development used.
  • Systems developed incrementally will, typically, have less detail in the requirements document (with in-house iterative development, details are added during development).
  • Critical systems need detailed requirements, because safety and security must be analysed; outsourced systems need detailed and precise specifications.
  • Requirements document standards have been designed, e.g. the IEEE standard. These are mostly applicable to the requirements for large systems engineering projects — such as military command-and-control systems with a long lifetime, developed by a group of organisations.

The structure of a requirements document

One possible organisation, based on the IEEE standard and extended with information about predicted system evolution:

A long document for a complex system built by several companies needs a full table of contents and index. A document for an in-house software product leaves out many of these chapters and focuses on the user requirements and high-level non-functional requirements.

4.6 Requirements validation Must know

Validation asks: "Have we written down the right system?" — before anyone starts building it. Catching a wrong requirement on paper is cheap; catching it after delivery is very expensive.

Requirements validation — the process of demonstrating that the requirements define the system that the customer really wants.

  • It overlaps with elicitation and analysis, as it is concerned with finding problems with the requirements.
  • Requirements error costs are high, so validation is very important: fixing a requirements error after delivery may cost up to 100 times the cost of fixing an implementation error.
  • Why so expensive? A requirements change usually means the system design and implementation must also change, and the system must then be retested.

Requirements checking Must know

CheckQuestion to askTextbook detail
ValidityDoes the system provide the functions which best support the customer's needs?Requirements reflect the real needs of users — which may have changed since they were elicited.
ConsistencyAre there any requirements conflicts?No contradictory constraints and no different descriptions of the same function.
CompletenessAre all functions required by the customer included?The document defines all functions and constraints intended by the system user.
RealismCan the requirements be implemented given available budget and technology?Use knowledge of existing technology; also consider the budget and schedule.
VerifiabilityCan the requirements be checked?Requirements are written so that tests can show the delivered system meets each one — this reduces disputes between customer and contractor.

V-C-C-R-V — "Very Careful Clients Request Verification": Validity, Consistency, Completeness, Realism, Verifiability.

Requirements validation techniques Must know

  1. Requirements reviews — systematic manual analysis of the requirements by a team of reviewers, who check for errors and inconsistencies.
  2. Prototyping — using an executable model of the system to check requirements (Chapter 2). End-users and customers experiment with it and feed back requirements changes.
  3. Test-case generation — developing tests for requirements to check testability. If a test is difficult or impossible to design, the requirement will usually be hard to implement and should be reconsidered. Writing tests from the user requirements before any code is an integral part of test-driven development.

Requirements reviews Must know

  • Regular reviews should be held while the requirements definition is being formulated.
  • Both client and contractor staff should be involved in reviews.
  • Reviews may be formal (with completed documents) or informal. Good communication between developers, customers and users can resolve problems at an early stage.
  • In a review, people from the customer and the developer read the document in detail and check for errors, anomalies and inconsistencies; once these are recorded, customer and developer negotiate how to solve them.

Review checks

Review checkQuestion
VerifiabilityIs the requirement realistically testable?
ComprehensibilityIs the requirement properly understood?
TraceabilityIs the origin of the requirement clearly stated?
AdaptabilityCan the requirement be changed without a large impact on other requirements?

Don't mix the two lists. Requirements checks (5): validity, consistency, completeness, realism, verifiability. Review checks (4): verifiability, comprehensibility, traceability, adaptability. Only verifiability appears in both.

Be realistic about validation. It is hard to show that requirements meet a user's needs: users must picture the system in operation and imagine how it fits their work — hard even for skilled professionals. You rarely find all problems during validation, so further changes to correct omissions and misunderstandings are inevitable after the document has been agreed.

4.7 Requirements change Must know

The requirements for large software systems are always changing. One reason is that these systems often address "wicked" problems — problems that cannot be completely defined — so the requirements are bound to be incomplete. During development, the stakeholders' understanding of the problem keeps changing, and the requirements must evolve to reflect it (Fig 4.12).

Why requirements change Must know

Once a system is installed and regularly used, new requirements inevitably emerge. Some come from errors and omissions in the original requirements, but most come from changes to the system's environment:

  1. The business and technical environment of the system always changes after installation. New hardware may be introduced, it may be necessary to interface the system with other systems, business priorities may change (with consequent changes in the system support required), and new legislation and regulations may be introduced that the system must necessarily abide by.
  2. The people who pay for a system and the users of that system are rarely the same people. System customers impose requirements because of organisational and budgetary constraints. These may conflict with end-user requirements and, after delivery, new features may have to be added for user support if the system is to meet its goals.
  3. Large systems usually have a diverse user community, with many users having different requirements and priorities that may be conflicting or contradictory. The final system requirements are inevitably a compromise between them and, with experience, it is often discovered that the balance of support given to different users has to be changed.

Requirements management Must know

Requirements management — the process of managing changing requirements during the requirements engineering process and system development.

  • New requirements emerge as a system is being developed and after it has gone into use.
  • You need to keep track of individual requirements and maintain links between dependent requirements, so that you can assess the impact of requirements changes.
  • You need to establish a formal process for making change proposals and linking these to system requirements. Requirements management should start as soon as a draft of the requirements document is available.
  • Agile processes handle change without a formal process: the user prioritises the change and, if it is high priority, decides which planned features to drop. The problem: users are not necessarily the best judges of whether a change is cost-effective. With many stakeholders, an independent authority that balances everyone's needs is often better.

Requirements management planning Must know

Planning establishes the level of requirements management detail that is required. The requirements management decisions are:

DecisionWhat it means
Requirements identificationEach requirement must be uniquely identified so that it can be cross-referenced with other requirements (and used in traceability assessments).
A change management processThe set of activities that assess the impact and cost of changes (Fig 4.13).
Traceability policiesDefine the relationships between each requirement, and between the requirements and the system design, that should be recorded — and how these records are maintained.
Tool supportTools may range from specialist requirements management systems to spreadsheets and simple database systems.

Tool support is needed for requirements storage (a secure, managed data store accessible to everyone involved), change management (tracking suggested changes and the responses to them) and traceability management (discovering related requirements). Small systems can manage with shared web documents, spreadsheets and databases; large systems use specialised tools such as DOORS.

Traceability is the "family tree" of each requirement: where it came from (its source), which other requirements depend on it, and which parts of the design implement it. When one requirement changes, you can follow the links to see how the change ripples through the system.

Requirements change management Must know

Change management is applied to all proposed changes after the requirements document has been approved. It decides whether a requirements change should be accepted — whether the benefits of the change justify its cost. A formal process means all change proposals are treated consistently and the document is changed in a controlled way.

  1. Problem analysis and change specification — the process starts with an identified requirements problem or a specific change proposal. During this stage, the problem or the change proposal is analysed to check that it is valid. This analysis is fed back to the change requestor, who may respond with a more specific requirements change proposal, or decide to withdraw the request.
  2. Change analysis and costing — the effect of the proposed change is assessed using traceability information and general knowledge of the system requirements. The cost is estimated in terms of changes to the requirements document and, if appropriate, to the design and implementation. Once this analysis is completed, a decision is made whether or not to proceed with the requirements change.
  3. Change implementation — the requirements document and, where necessary, the system design and implementation are modified. Ideally, the document should be organised so that changes can be easily implemented (modular sections, few external references).

Emergency changes: there is always a temptation to change the system first and update the requirements document later. The document and the implementation then get out of step, because changes are easily forgotten. If an emergency change is unavoidable, update the requirements document as soon as possible.

4.8 Lab: analysing a real-time case study Important

The lab asks you to analyse the requirements of a real-time application — software that must react to its inputs within fixed time limits. The textbook's insulin pump is a good model: an embedded system that collects information from a sensor and controls a pump that delivers a controlled dose of insulin. It is safety-critical: if it fails, the user's health may be damaged. Use the same steps for any case study (weather station, traffic signal, ATM, …).

  1. Understand the system and its environment — read the case; sketch the hardware and the flow of data (Fig 4.14). Note the timing constraints.
  2. Identify the stakeholders and the type of each (end users, managers, owners, external).
  3. Choose elicitation techniques — interviews, observation, stories/scenarios — and say why.
  4. Write user requirements — high-level, in natural language.
  5. Write functional system requirements — numbered, "shall", with a rationale.
  6. Write non-functional requirements — classify each (product / organisational / external) and make it measurable (metrics table).
  7. Specify one critical function as a structured form or table; add a scenario or use-case diagram.
  8. Validate and plan for change — apply the five checks; give every requirement an ID; mark enduring vs volatile ones.

  • Why change: environment changes · payers ≠ users · diverse users. RM planning: identification, change process, traceability policies, tool support. Change mgmt: problem analysis & change spec → change analysis & costing → change implementation.
