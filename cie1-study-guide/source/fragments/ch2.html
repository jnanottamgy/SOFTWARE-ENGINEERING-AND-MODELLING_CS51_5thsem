2.1 The software process Must know

Every piece of software is produced by people following some sequence of steps: find out what is needed, decide how to build it, build it, check it, and keep changing it afterwards. That organised sequence of steps is the software process.

Software process — a structured set of activities required to develop a software system. (Textbook: a set of related activities that leads to the production of a software system.)

The four fundamental activities Must know

There are many different software processes, but all of them involve these four activities:

ActivityWhat it means (slide wording)
1. SpecificationDefining what the system should do (its functionality and the constraints on its operation).
2. Design and implementationDefining the organisation of the system and implementing the system (textbook: software development).
3. ValidationChecking that it does what the customer wants.
4. EvolutionChanging the system in response to changing customer needs.

Each activity has sub-activities (e.g. requirements validation, architectural design, unit testing), and processes also contain supporting activities such as configuration management and project planning.

S-D-V-E — "Some Developers Value Evolution": Specification, Design & implementation, Validation, Evolution.

Software process models and process descriptions Must know

Software process model — an abstract representation of a process. It presents a description of a process from some particular perspective.

A process model is also called a Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) model; the very general models are called process paradigms. Being a simplified view from one perspective, a model shows only part of the process (e.g. an activity model shows activities and their order, but not who does them). Companies adapt these general frameworks into their own processes.

When we describe a process we usually talk about its activities (e.g. specifying a data model, designing a user interface) and the ordering of these activities. A process description may also include:

ElementMeaningExample
ProductsThe outcomes of a process activity.Architectural design produces a model of the software architecture.
RolesThe responsibilities of the people involved in the process.Project manager, configuration manager, programmer.
Pre- and post-conditionsStatements that are true before and after a process activity has been enacted or a product produced.Pre: customer has approved all requirements. Post: the architecture models have been reviewed.

Plan-driven and agile processes Must know

Plan-driven processes — processes where all of the process activities are planned in advance and progress is measured against this plan.

Agile processes — planning is incremental, and it is easier to change the process to reflect changing customer requirements.

PointPlan-drivenAgile
PlanningAll activities planned in advance; progress measured against the planIncremental and continual as the software is developed
ChangeCostly: plans and documents must be redoneEasy to change the process to follow the customer
RequirementsComplete specification before design startsSpecified informally for each increment just before it is built
Documents & testingDetailed design documents; testing driven by test plans and an independent test teamMinimum documentation; design kept in the code; tests written with the requirements
SuitsSafety-critical, embedded, large multi-site systems (e.g. waterfall, V-model)Business systems and products with rapidly changing requirements (Chapter 3)

There are no right or wrong software processes — no single process suits every system. The choice depends on the type of software, the customer's requirements and the developers' skills: a safety-critical system needs a very structured process with detailed records; a business system with fast-changing requirements suits a flexible agile process. In practice, most practical processes include elements of both plan-driven and agile approaches.

2.2 Software process models (Sommerville) Must know

ModelKey idea (slide wording)Plan-driven or agile?
The waterfall modelSeparate and distinct phases of specification and development.Plan-driven
Incremental developmentSpecification, development and validation are interleaved.May be plan-driven or agile

In practice, most large systems are developed using a process that incorporates elements from all of these models: well-understood parts are built waterfall-style or bought and configured, hard-to-specify parts are developed incrementally, and components are reused throughout (the architecture, though, must be planned up front). Typically, safety-critical software uses waterfall, software products are incremental, and business systems are configured from existing systems. Read once: the Rational Unified Process (RUP) tries to combine all the general models; its phases are inception, elaboration, construction and transition.

The waterfall model Must know

Water in a waterfall falls from one ledge to the next and never flows back up. Each phase "cascades" into the next: finish and sign off the requirements, then design, then code, then test, then deliver.

The first published process model (Royce, 1970), taken from large military systems engineering; also called the software life cycle. It is plan-driven: you plan and schedule all activities before starting development.

  1. Requirements analysis and definition — the system's services, constraints and goals are established with users and defined in detail as the system specification.
  2. System and software design — requirements are allocated to hardware or software and an overall architecture is established; software design identifies the fundamental software abstractions and their relationships.
  3. Implementation and unit testing — the design is realised as programs or program units; each unit is verified against its specification.
  4. Integration and system testing — units are integrated and tested as a complete system against the requirements, then delivered to the customer.
  5. Operation and maintenance — normally the longest phase: the system is used; maintenance corrects errors not found earlier, improves the implementation and adds new services.

The result of each phase is one or more approved ("signed-off") documents, and a phase has to be complete before moving onto the next. In real software, phases overlap and feed information back (dashed lines in Fig 2.1). The main drawback is the difficulty of accommodating change after the process is underway.

R-S-I-I-O — "Rain Slides Into Icy Oceans": Requirements, System design, Implementation, Integration, Operation.

When to use it: embedded systems (hardware is inflexible, so software decisions can't be delayed); critical systems (need safety and security analysis of a complete specification and design); and large systems engineering projects developed at several sites or by partner companies, where its plan-driven nature helps coordinate the work. Avoid it when requirements change quickly and the team can communicate informally. Read once: formal system development (e.g. the B method) is a waterfall variant that transforms a mathematical specification into code; used only for very critical systems.

10 marks: diagram with feedback lines → one line per phase → "plan-driven, signed-off documents" → 3 advantages, 3 problems → when appropriate, with an example (insulin pump).

Advantages

  • Simple, systematic; easy to plan and manage
  • Signed-off documents make progress visible
  • Plan-driven nature coordinates work at several sites
  • Complete specification allows safety/security analysis

Problems

  • Inflexible partitioning into distinct stages makes it difficult to respond to changing customer requirements
  • Only appropriate when requirements are well understood and changes limited — few business systems have stable requirements
  • Requirements get frozen too early, so the system may not do what users want
  • Pressman: customers can't state all requirements up front; no working version until late; team members wait in "blocking states"

Incremental development Must know

Incremental development — develop an initial implementation, get feedback from users, and evolve the software through several versions until the required system is developed. Specification, development and validation are interleaved, with rapid feedback across activities.

Now the most common approach for application systems and software products, and the basis of agile methods. It can be plan-driven (increments identified in advance), agile (later increments depend on progress and priorities) or a mix. Early increments hold the most important or urgent functionality, so the customer evaluates the system early; if it is wrong, only the current increment changes.

Like writing an essay in drafts: write a rough version, show it, improve it, show it again — each draft (increment) adds to the last.

Benefits

  • Cost of accommodating changing requirements is reduced — far less analysis and documentation to redo than with waterfall.
  • Easier to get customer feedback — customers comment on demonstrations and see how much has been implemented.
  • More rapid delivery and deployment of useful software — customers gain value earlier than with waterfall.

Problems

  • The process is not visible — managers need regular deliverables to measure progress; documenting every quickly-built version is not cost-effective.
  • System structure tends to degrade as increments are added. Unless time and money is spent on refactoring, regular change corrupts the structure and further changes become difficult and costly.

These problems are worst for large, long-lifetime systems built by several teams, which need a stable architecture planned in advance.

Incremental development does not mean every increment is delivered to the customer — versions may only be shown for comment. Deploying increments for real use is incremental delivery (Section 2.5.2).

Integration and configuration (reuse-oriented SE) Must know

Like furnishing a room with ready-made furniture you assemble and adjust, instead of making every piece from raw wood.

  • Based on software reuse: systems are integrated from existing components or application systems, sometimes called COTS (commercial-off-the-shelf) systems.
  • Reused elements may be configured to adapt their behaviour and functionality to a user's requirements.
  • Reuse is now the standard approach for building many types of business system (covered in depth in Sommerville Chapter 15).

Types of reusable software: (1) stand-alone application systems (COTS) configured for a particular environment; (2) collections of objects developed as a package to be integrated with a component framework such as .NET or J2EE (textbook: Java Spring); (3) web services developed to service standards and available for remote invocation.

  1. Requirements specification — brief initial requirements: essential requirements and desirable features.
  2. Software discovery and evaluation — search for components/systems providing the functionality and evaluate whether they meet the essential requirements.
  3. Requirements refinement — modify the requirements to fit the components found; if impossible, search again (so requirements engineering is repeated).
  4. Application system configuration — if an off-the-shelf system meets the requirements, configure it to create the new system.
  5. Component adaptation and integration — otherwise, modify reusable components, develop new ones, and integrate them.

Advantages

  • Reduced costs and risks as less software is developed from scratch
  • Faster delivery and deployment of the system

Disadvantages

  • Requirements compromises are inevitable, so the system may not meet the real needs of users
  • Loss of control over evolution of reused system elements

iLearn (digital learning environment for schools) is built this way: its application services (email, conferencing, spreadsheets, a virtual learning environment) are bought or freely available, and configuration services adapt it to each school.

2.3 More process models (from Textbook 2, Pressman) Must know

These models are not in Sommerville's chapter or your slides (except the V-model, slide 30) but are commonly examined. Pressman calls them prescriptive process models, and labels his diagrams with five generic framework activities: Communication (requirements gathering), Planning (estimating, scheduling, tracking, risk analysis), Modeling (analysis, design), Construction (code, test) and Deployment (delivery, support, feedback). His waterfall ("classic life cycle") is these five in sequence.

The V-model Must know

  • A variation of the waterfall model showing how quality-assurance (verification and validation) actions relate to the earlier communication, modeling and construction actions.
  • Going down the left side, requirements are refined into more detailed technical representations; after code is generated, the team goes up the right side, running tests that validate each model made on the way down.
  • There is no fundamental difference between the classic life cycle and the V-model; the V simply visualises where V&V is applied. Pros: tests planned early, clear stage-to-test link. Cons: as inflexible as waterfall; working software late.
  • Sommerville's version (your slide 30) adds the test plans — Fig 2.10 in Section 2.4.3.

Pressman's incremental model (read once) applies linear sequences in a staggered way over calendar time; each delivers an operational increment, the first usually a core product (e.g. a word processor: basic editing first, then advanced editing, then spell-checking, then page layout). Because each increment is delivered to the customer, it matches Sommerville's incremental delivery.

Evolutionary model 1: the prototyping paradigm Must know

Evolutionary models are iterative: each iteration produces a more complete version. Use prototyping when the customer has general objectives but no detailed requirements, or the developer is unsure of an algorithm's efficiency, an operating system's adaptability or the form of the user interaction. It can be a stand-alone model but is more often a technique used inside other models.

  1. Communication — define overall objectives, known requirements and areas needing definition.
  2. Quick plan — plan an iteration quickly.
  3. Modeling (quick design) — of what users will see: screen layouts, output formats.
  4. Construction of prototype — build quickly, reusing fragments and tools.
  5. Deployment, delivery & feedback — stakeholders evaluate; feedback refines the requirements. Iterate.

A throwaway prototype is discarded after use (Brooks: "plan to throw one away"); an evolutionary prototype slowly grows into the real system. Strengths: clarifies fuzzy requirements; users get an early feel for the system; developers build something at once. Problems: (1) stakeholders see an apparently working version, unaware it is held together haphazardly, and demand "a few fixes" to ship it; (2) quick implementation compromises (wrong language, inefficient algorithm) become a permanent part of the system.

Define the rules of the game at the beginning: everyone agrees the prototype only defines requirements; it is then discarded (at least in part) and the real software is engineered for quality. Sommerville's prototyping (slides 37–41) is in Section 2.5.1.

Evolutionary model 2: the spiral model (Boehm) Must know

Like climbing a spiral staircase: on each round you pass the same points again (communicate, plan and analyse risks, design, build, deliver), but each round takes you higher — to a more complete system — and you check for risks every time.

Proposed by Barry Boehm (1988): an evolutionary, risk-driven model that couples the iterative nature of prototyping with the controlled, systematic aspects of the waterfall model. Its two distinguishing features: (1) a cyclic approach that grows the system's definition and implementation while decreasing its degree of risk; (2) anchor point milestones that secure stakeholder commitment to feasible, mutually satisfactory solutions.

  • The team moves clockwise from the centre through the framework activities; risk is considered on each revolution and anchor point milestones are noted on each pass.
  • Early circuits produce a specification or prototype; later circuits produce progressively more complete versions. Each pass through planning adjusts cost, schedule and the number of iterations.
  • Sommerville: each loop represents a phase (innermost — feasibility, next — requirements definition, next — system design, …). It combines change avoidance with change tolerance and has explicit risk management.
  • It can cover the whole life of the software (concept development → new product development → product enhancement) until the software is retired.

Also seen in exam answers: Boehm's original drawing (used in older Sommerville editions) splits each loop into four sectors — objective setting; risk assessment and reduction; development and validation; planning. Either drawing earns marks if labelled.

Advantages

  • Realistic approach for large-scale systems
  • Developer and customer understand and react to risks at each level
  • Prototyping used as risk reduction at any stage
  • Systematic stepwise approach inside an iterative framework

Disadvantages

  • Hard to convince customers (in contracts) it is controllable
  • Demands considerable risk-assessment expertise
  • If a major risk is missed, problems will occur
  • Poor fit for fixed budgets: cost revised every circuit

Read once — the concurrent model (concurrent engineering): all activities exist at the same time, each in a state (inactive, under development, awaiting changes, under review, under revision, baselined, done); events trigger transitions, e.g. a requirements error found during design moves requirements analysis from "done" to "awaiting changes". It gives an accurate picture of a project's current state.

2.4 Process activities Must know

Real software processes are inter-leaved sequences of technical, collaborative and managerial activities with the overall goal of specifying, designing, implementing and testing a software system (usually with tool support).

The four basic activities — specification, development, validation and evolution — are organised differently in different processes: in the waterfall model they are in sequence, whereas in incremental development they are interleaved.

2.4.1 Software specification Must know

Software specification — the process of establishing what services are required and the constraints on the system's operation and development. This is requirements engineering: the process of developing a software specification.

It is a critical stage: mistakes here cause later problems in design and implementation. A short, cheap feasibility study may come first (is there a need, and is it technically and financially realistic?). The goal is an agreed requirements document at two levels: user requirements for customers and end-users, and detailed system requirements for developers.

ActivitySlide pointTextbook detail
Requirements elicitation and analysisWhat do the system stakeholders require or expect from the system?Observe existing systems, talk to users and procurers, task analysis; may use models and prototypes.
Requirements specificationDefining the requirements in detail.Write the gathered information as user requirements and system requirements.
Requirements validationChecking the validity of the requirements.Check realism, consistency and completeness; correct the errors found.

The activities are interleaved (back-arrows in Fig 2.7). In agile methods specification is not separate: requirements are written informally for each increment just before it is built. Example (insulin pump): "The system shall be available to deliver insulin when required" is a high-level user requirement.

2.4.2 Software design and implementation Must know

Design and implementation — the process of converting the system specification into an executable system.

Software design: design a software structure that realises the specification (structure, data, interfaces between components, sometimes algorithms). Implementation: translate this structure into an executable program. The two are closely related and may be inter-leaved; in agile development design is informal (whiteboards) with no formal design documents.

Inputs: platform information (the operating system, database, middleware and other systems the software runs with), the requirements specification, and a data description. Outputs: detailed documents for critical systems, design diagrams in model-driven development, or just the code in agile development.

Design activityWhat you do
Architectural designIdentify the overall structure of the system, the principal components (sub-systems or modules), their relationships and how they are distributed.
Database designDesign the system data structures and how these are to be represented in a database.
Interface designDefine the interfaces between system components — unambiguously, so a component can be used without knowing its implementation.
Component selection and designSearch for reusable components; if unavailable, design how the new component will operate.

The activities are interleaved and interdependent, so design rework is inevitable; they also vary (a real-time system adds timing design and may have no database).

System implementation Important

  • The software is implemented either by developing a program or programs or by configuring an application system. Design and implementation are interleaved for most systems.
  • Programming is an individual activity with no standard process (some programmers start with familiar components, others leave them till last).
  • Debugging is the activity of finding program faults and correcting these faults — by forming and testing hypotheses about the program's behaviour, often with interactive debuggers.

Testing ≠ debugging. Testing establishes the existence of defects; debugging locates and corrects them.

2.4.3 Software validation Must know

Verification and validation (V & V) — is intended to show that a system conforms to its specification and meets the requirements of the system customer.

In short, validation checks that the system conforms to its specification and meets the real needs of its users. V & V involves checking and review processes and system testing. System testing involves executing the system with test cases that are derived from the specification of the real data to be processed by the system. Testing is the most commonly used V & V activity. Except for small programs, systems are tested in stages, not as one unit.

StageSlide pointsTextbook detail
Component testingIndividual components are tested independently; components may be functions or objects or coherent groupings of these.Done by the developers; tools such as JUnit rerun tests automatically.
System testingTesting of the system as a whole. Testing of emergent properties is particularly important.Finds interaction and interface errors; checks functional and non-functional requirements.
Customer testingTesting with customer data to check that the system meets the customer's needs.Last stage before use; may reveal requirements problems. For products: beta testing by selected users.

Emergent properties belong to the whole system, not to any one part (e.g. overall reliability, performance) — so they can only be tested after integration.

Testing phases in a plan-driven software process (V-model) Must know

In a plan-driven process (e.g. for critical systems), an independent team of testers works from test plans developed from the system specification and design. The test plans are the link between development and testing; the V-model shows the validation activity that corresponds to each stage of the waterfall model.

Read it as a V lying on its side: development runs along the top to module and unit code and test; testing returns along the bottom to service. Requirements + system specification → acceptance test plan; system specification + system design → system integration test plan; system design + detailed design → sub-system integration test plan. (The 10th-edition textbook says "customer test plan", "component design" and "component code and test" — either labelling is correct.)

2.4.4 Software evolution Must know

  • Software is inherently flexible and can change — far more cheaply than hardware, even after delivery.
  • As requirements change through changing business circumstances, the software that supports the business must also evolve and change — it must evolve to remain useful.
  • Although there has been a demarcation between development and evolution (maintenance), this is increasingly irrelevant as fewer and fewer systems are completely new; development and maintenance form one continuum.

2.5 Coping with change Must know

Change is inevitable in all large software projects: business changes lead to new and changed system requirements; new technologies open up new possibilities for improving implementations; changing platforms require application changes. Change leads to rework (redoing completed work), so the costs of change include both rework (e.g. re-analysing requirements) and the costs of implementing new functionality.

Change anticipationChange tolerance
MeaningThe process includes activities that can anticipate possible changes before significant rework is required.The process is designed so that changes can be accommodated at relatively low cost.
ExampleA prototype system shows key features to customers, who refine their requirements before costly development.Incremental development: changes go into increments not yet developed; if impossible, only a single increment (a small part of the system) is altered.
TechniqueSystem prototypingIncremental delivery (also gives change avoidance); refactoring

So there are two ways of coping with changing requirements: system prototyping — a version of the system or part of it is developed quickly to check the customer's requirements and the feasibility of design decisions (supports change anticipation); and incremental delivery — increments are delivered to the customer for comment and experimentation (supports both change avoidance and change tolerance). Processes structured for iterative development and delivery let changes be made without disrupting the system as a whole.

2.5.1 Software prototyping Must know

Prototype — an initial version of a system used to demonstrate concepts and try out design options.

A prototype can be used in

  1. Requirements engineering — to help with requirements elicitation and validation.
  2. Design — to explore options and develop a UI design (rapid prototyping with users is the only sensible way to develop user interfaces).
  3. Testing — to run back-to-back tests: the same tests run on the prototype and the system, and the outputs are compared.

Benefits of prototyping

  1. Improved system usability
  2. A closer match to users' real needs
  3. Improved design quality
  4. Improved maintainability
  5. Reduced development effort

Objectives must be explicit (e.g. develop the UI, validate functional requirements, or demo to managers) — one prototype rarely meets all of them. Functionality: decide what to leave out. Evaluation: train users and let them settle into normal use; note that users may not use a prototype the way they will use the final system. Prototype development (slide 40):

  • May be based on rapid prototyping languages or tools.
  • May involve leaving out functionality: focus on areas of the product that are not well understood; error checking and recovery may not be included; focus on functional rather than non-functional requirements such as reliability and security.

Throw-away prototypes

Prototypes should be discarded after development, as they are not a good basis for a production system:

  1. It may be impossible to tune the system to meet non-functional requirements.
  2. Prototypes are normally undocumented.
  3. The prototype structure is usually degraded through rapid change.
  4. The prototype probably will not meet normal organisational quality standards.

Your manager wants to ship your impressive prototype as the production system. What do you say?Give the four throw-away reasons: it cannot be tuned for performance, security or reliability; it has no documentation; its structure is degraded, so it is hard to maintain; it fails quality standards. Use it as a specification and build the real system properly.

2.5.2 Incremental delivery Must know

Incremental delivery — rather than deliver the system as a single delivery, the development and delivery is broken down into increments, with each increment delivering part of the required functionality.

User requirements are prioritised and the highest priority requirements are included in early increments. Once the development of an increment is started, its requirements are frozen, though requirements for later increments can continue to evolve. Each finished increment is installed in the customer's working environment and integrated with earlier ones.

Advantages

  • Customer value can be delivered with each increment, so system functionality is available earlier.
  • Early increments act as a prototype to help elicit requirements for later increments (and they are part of the real system — no relearning).
  • Lower risk of overall project failure.
  • The highest priority system services tend to receive the most testing.

Problems

  • Most systems need basic facilities used by different parts; as requirements are not detailed until an increment is implemented, these common facilities are hard to identify.
  • In iterative processes the specification is developed with the software; this conflicts with the procurement model of many organisations, where the complete specification is part of the contract.
  • Hard for replacement systems — users won't accept less than the old system.

Incremental development vs incremental delivery Must know

PointIncremental developmentIncremental delivery
Each incrementDevelop the system in increments and evaluate each increment before proceeding to the nextDeploy each increment for use by end-users

Incremental development is about how you build (in pieces, with feedback). Incremental delivery is about how you release (every piece goes into real use). Delivery implies incremental development, but not the other way round.

2.6 Process improvement Must know

Many software companies have turned to software process improvement as a way of enhancing the quality of their software, reducing costs or accelerating their development processes.

Process improvement — understanding existing processes and changing these processes to increase product quality and/or reduce costs and development time.

Process maturity approachAgile approach
FocusImproving process and project management and introducing good software engineering practiceIterative development and the reduction of overheads in the software process
Key ideaThe level of process maturity reflects the extent to which good technical and management practice has been adopted in the organisation's processesThe primary characteristics of agile methods are rapid delivery of functionality and responsiveness to changing customer requirements
Goal / styleProduct quality and process predictability; plan-driven, adds "overhead" activitiesBest processes have the lowest overheads; minimal formality and documentation

The process improvement cycle Must know

  1. Process measurement — measure one or more attributes of the process or product. These measurements form a baseline to decide if improvements have been effective.
  2. Process analysis — assess the current process; identify weaknesses and bottlenecks. Process models (process maps) may be developed.
  3. Process change — propose and introduce changes for the weaknesses; the cycle resumes to collect data on their effectiveness.

Process measurement: wherever possible, quantitative process data should be collected — but where organisations do not have clearly defined process standards this is very difficult, as you don't know what to measure; a process may have to be defined first. Measurements should be used to assess improvements, but should not drive them: the improvement driver should be the organisational objectives.

Capability maturity levels — the SEI capability maturity model Must know

Its five maturity levels essentially correspond to the use of good software engineering practice. The model later evolved into CMMI (slide 54 names).

Chapter 2 in one page

Definitions

  • Software process: structured set of activities required to develop a software system.
  • Process model: abstract representation of a process from a particular perspective.
  • Plan-driven: all activities planned in advance; progress measured against the plan. Agile: incremental planning; easy to change.
  • Prototype: initial version used to demonstrate concepts and try out design options.
  • Process improvement: understand and change processes to raise quality and/or reduce cost and time.

Lists to write in full

  • Activities: specification, design & implementation, validation, evolution.
  • Process description: products, roles, pre-/post-conditions.
  • Waterfall: requirements; system & software design; implementation & unit test; integration & system test; operation & maintenance.
  • Reusable software: COTS apps; object collections (.NET/J2EE); web services.
  • Reuse stages: spec; discovery & evaluation; refinement; configuration; adaptation & integration.
  • RE: elicitation & analysis; specification; validation.
  • Design: architectural, database, interface, component selection & design.
  • Testing: component, system, customer (acceptance).
  • Prototyping uses: RE, design, back-to-back testing. Benefits: usability, real needs, design quality, maintainability, less effort.

Comparisons

  • Waterfall vs incremental: sequential vs interleaved; change cost; feedback; visibility.
  • Incr. development vs delivery: evaluate with proxy vs deploy to real users.
  • Anticipation vs tolerance: prototyping vs incremental.
  • Maturity vs agile improvement: good practice vs low overhead.
  • Testing vs debugging: find defects vs locate and fix them.

Numbers and names

  • Incremental development: 3 benefits, 2 problems. Incremental delivery: 4 advantages, 2 problems.
  • Throw-away reasons: NFRs untunable; undocumented; degraded structure; below standards.
  • Improvement cycle: measure → analyse → change. Metrics: time, resources, events.
  • SEI CMM: Initial, Repeatable, Defined, Managed, Optimising.
  • Spiral: Boehm 1988, risk-driven. Waterfall: Royce 1970.

Diagrams to practise

  • Waterfall · incremental · reuse · both V-models · prototyping paradigm · spiral
  • RE process · design process · testing stages · evolution
  • Prototype development · incremental delivery · improvement cycle · CMM staircase

Case studies

  • Pump → waterfall + V · Mentcare → incremental + prototyping · Weather station → plan-driven in a spiral · iLearn → reuse + incremental.
