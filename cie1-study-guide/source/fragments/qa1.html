Q1 What is software engineering? What are the essential attributes of good software? List the key challenges faced in software engineering.

5 marksCO1L2Asked in CIE-1 2024Very likely
Definition 1 + Four attributes 2 + Challenges 2
Model answer

Software engineering is an engineering discipline that is concerned with all aspects of software production, from the early stages of system specification through to maintaining the system after it has gone into use.

  • Engineering discipline: using appropriate theories and methods to solve problems, bearing in mind organisational and financial constraints.
  • All aspects of software production: not just the technical process of development, but also project management and the development of tools, methods and theories. (Software = computer programs and associated documentation.)

Essential attributes of good software. Good software should deliver the required functionality and performance to the user and should be maintainable, dependable and usable:

  1. Maintainability: written so that it can evolve to meet the changing needs of customers, because software change is inevitable in a changing business environment.
  2. Dependability and security: includes reliability, security and safety; it should not cause physical or economic damage if the system fails, and malicious users should not be able to access or damage it.
  3. Efficiency: no wasteful use of system resources such as memory and processor cycles (responsiveness, processing time, memory utilisation).
  4. Acceptability: acceptable to the type of users it is designed for, i.e. understandable, usable and compatible with the other systems they use.

Key challenges. Four general issues affect many types of software and create its main challenges:

ChallengeMeaning
1. HeterogeneitySystems must operate as distributed systems across networks of different types of computers and mobile devices, and integrate with older legacy systems.
2. Business and social changeBusiness and society change very quickly, so existing software must be changed and new software developed rapidly.
3. Security and trustSoftware is intertwined with all aspects of our lives, so we must be able to trust it, especially remote systems used through web pages or web services.
4. ScaleSoftware is built at very different scales, from very small embedded systems in wearable devices to Internet-scale, cloud-based systems serving a global community.

In short, the key challenges are coping with increasing diversity, demands for reduced delivery times and developing trustworthy software.

"My Dog Eats Apples" = Maintainability, Dependability & security, Efficiency, Acceptability. "Hungry Bears Steal Snacks" = Heterogeneity, Business & social change, Security & trust, Scale.

Q2 Explain the ACM/IEEE Code of Ethics. What are the issues of professional responsibility?

5 marksCO1L2Likely
Ethics intro 1 + Professional responsibility 1.5 + Code and eight principles 2.5
Model answer

Software engineering involves wider responsibilities than simply the application of technical skills. Software engineers must behave in an honest and ethically responsible way if they are to be respected as professionals. Ethical behaviour is more than simply upholding the law: it involves following a set of principles that are morally correct.

Issues of professional responsibility (areas where acceptable behaviour is set by professional responsibility, not by laws):

  1. Confidentiality: respect the confidentiality of employers or clients, whether or not a formal confidentiality agreement has been signed.
  2. Competence: do not misrepresent your level of competence or knowingly accept work that is outside your competence.
  3. Intellectual property rights: be aware of local laws on the use of intellectual property such as patents and copyright, and protect the intellectual property of employers and clients.
  4. Computer misuse: do not use technical skills to misuse other people's computers. Misuse ranges from trivial (game playing on an employer's machine) to extremely serious (dissemination of viruses).

ACM/IEEE Code of Ethics. The professional societies in the US, ACM and IEEE, cooperated to produce the Software Engineering Code of Ethics and Professional Practice. Members of these organisations sign up to the code when they join. Because software engineers have significant opportunities to do good or cause harm, they commit to making software engineering a beneficial and respected profession. The code contains eight principles on the behaviour and decisions of professional software engineers, including practitioners, educators, managers, supervisors, policy makers, trainees and students. In accordance with their commitment to the health, safety and welfare of the public, software engineers shall adhere to:

PrincipleSoftware engineers shall …
1. Publicact consistently with the public interest.
2. Client and employeract in a manner that is in the best interests of their client and employer consistent with the public interest.
3. Productensure that their products and related modifications meet the highest professional standards possible.
4. Judgmentmaintain integrity and independence in their professional judgment.
5. Management(managers and leaders) subscribe to and promote an ethical approach to the management of software development and maintenance.
6. Professionadvance the integrity and reputation of the profession consistent with the public interest.
7. Colleaguesbe fair to and supportive of their colleagues.
8. Selfparticipate in lifelong learning regarding the practice of their profession and promote an ethical approach to the practice of the profession.

Principles in pairs: PC · PJ · MP · CS, "A PC in PJs meets an MP from CS" = Public–Client, Product–Judgment, Management–Profession, Colleagues–Self. Responsibility issues: "Careful Coders Inspect Code" = Confidentiality, Competence, IPR, Computer misuse.

Q3 Explain the waterfall model with a neat diagram. When is it appropriate, and what are its problems?

5 marksCO1L2Very likely
Diagram 2 + Five phases 1.5 + When appropriate and problems 1.5
Model answer

The waterfall model is a plan-driven process model with separate and distinct phases of specification and development: all process activities are planned and scheduled before development starts, and each phase must be complete before the next begins. It was the first published process model (Royce, 1970) and is also called the software life cycle.

  1. Requirements analysis and definition: the system's services, constraints and goals are established with users and defined in detail as the system specification.
  2. System and software design: requirements are allocated to hardware or software, an overall architecture is established, and the fundamental software abstractions and their relationships are identified.
  3. Implementation and unit testing: the design is realised as a set of programs or program units; each unit is verified against its specification.
  4. Integration and system testing: units are integrated and tested as a complete system against the requirements, then delivered to the customer.
  5. Operation and maintenance: normally the longest phase; the system is used, and maintenance corrects errors, improves the implementation and adds new services.

The result of each phase is one or more approved (signed-off) documents. In practice the phases overlap and feed information back (dashed arrows). The main drawback is the difficulty of accommodating change after the process is underway.

When it is appropriate

  • Requirements are well understood and changes will be fairly limited
  • Embedded systems: software must interface with inflexible hardware, so decisions cannot be delayed
  • Critical systems: safety and security analysis needs a complete specification and design (e.g. insulin pump)
  • Large systems engineering projects developed at several sites: the plan-driven nature helps coordinate the work

Problems

  • Inflexible partitioning of the project into distinct stages makes it difficult to respond to changing customer requirements
  • Few business systems have stable requirements, so the model suits few of them
  • Requirements are frozen early, so the system may not do what users want
  • No working version until late; customers find it hard to state all requirements at the start

Marks go to the labelled diagram with feedback arrows, one line per phase, the words "plan-driven" and "signed-off documents", and an example system for "when appropriate".

Q4 With a neat diagram describe the incremental development model. What are the advantages and problems of the incremental approach?

5 marksCO1L2Asked in CIE-1 2024Very likely
Definition 1 + Diagram 2 + Advantages and problems 2
Model answer

Incremental development is based on the idea of developing an initial implementation, getting feedback from users and evolving the software through several versions until the required system has been developed. Specification, development and validation are interleaved rather than separate, with rapid feedback across activities.

  • Starting from an outline description, specification, development and validation run concurrently and produce an initial version, intermediate versions and finally the final version.
  • Each increment adds functionality. Early increments include the most important or most urgently required functionality, so the customer can evaluate the system early; if it is wrong, only the current increment has to be changed.
  • It is now the most common approach for application systems and software products and the basis of agile methods. It may be plan-driven (increments identified in advance), agile, or a mixture.

Advantages

  • Cost of accommodating changing customer requirements is reduced: far less analysis and documentation to redo than with waterfall
  • Easier to get customer feedback on the work done: customers comment on demonstrations and see how much has been implemented
  • More rapid delivery and deployment of useful software, so customers gain value earlier

Problems

  • The process is not visible: managers need regular deliverables to measure progress, and documenting every quickly built version is not cost-effective
  • System structure tends to degrade as new increments are added; unless time and money is spent on refactoring, regular change corrupts the structure and further changes become difficult and costly

These problems are most serious for large, long-lifetime systems developed by several teams, which need a stable architecture planned in advance.

Do not confuse this with incremental delivery: in incremental development an increment may only be demonstrated to the customer for comment; in incremental delivery each increment is deployed for real use.

Q5 How do software processes cope with change? Explain software prototyping (with the process of prototype development) and incremental delivery.

5 marksCO1L2Likely
Two approaches 1 + Prototyping with diagram 2 + Incremental delivery 2
Model answer

Change is inevitable in all large software projects: business changes lead to new and changed requirements, new technologies open up new possibilities, and changing platforms require application changes. Change leads to rework, so processes reduce its cost in two ways:

  • Change anticipation: the process includes activities that can anticipate possible changes before significant rework is required. Technique: system prototyping.
  • Change tolerance: the process is designed so that changes can be accommodated at relatively low cost. Technique: incremental delivery (changes go into increments not yet developed).

(a) Software prototyping. A prototype is an initial version of a system used to demonstrate concepts and try out design options. It is used in requirements engineering (elicitation and validation), in design (to explore options and develop the user interface) and in testing (back-to-back tests).

Prototype development may use rapid prototyping tools and leaves out functionality: it focuses on areas that are not well understood and on functional rather than non-functional requirements; error checking may be left out. Benefits: improved system usability, a closer match to users' real needs, improved design quality, improved maintainability and reduced development effort. Throw-away prototypes should be discarded, as they are undocumented, have a degraded structure and cannot be tuned to meet non-functional requirements.

(b) Incremental delivery. Rather than deliver the system as a single delivery, development and delivery are broken down into increments, each delivering part of the required functionality. User requirements are prioritised and the highest-priority ones go into early increments. Once an increment is started, its requirements are frozen; requirements for later increments can continue to evolve.

Advantages

  • Customer value is delivered with each increment, so functionality is available earlier
  • Early increments act as a prototype to help elicit requirements for later ones
  • Lower risk of overall project failure
  • The highest-priority services receive the most testing

Problems

  • Common basic facilities needed by different parts are hard to identify early
  • Conflicts with procurement models where the complete specification is part of the contract
  • Hard to use for replacement systems: users won't accept less than the old system

Q6 Explain the process improvement cycle and the SEI capability maturity levels with a neat diagram.

5 marksCO1L2Likely
Definition 1 + Improvement cycle 1.5 + Five maturity levels with diagram 2.5
Model answer

Process improvement means understanding existing processes and changing these processes to increase product quality and/or reduce costs and development time.

There are two approaches: the process maturity approach (improve process and project management and introduce good software engineering practice; the maturity level reflects how far good technical and management practice has been adopted) and the agile approach (iterative development and the reduction of overheads in the process).

  1. Process measurement: measure one or more attributes of the process or product; these form a baseline to decide whether improvements have been effective.
  2. Process analysis: assess the current process and identify weaknesses and bottlenecks; process models (process maps) may be developed.
  3. Process change: propose and introduce changes for the weaknesses found; the cycle then resumes to collect data on their effectiveness.

Measurements (time taken, resources required, number of occurrences of an event such as defects found) should be used to assess improvements but should not drive them: the driver should be the organisational objectives.

SEI capability maturity levels. In the late 1980s the Software Engineering Institute (SEI) proposed a model of process capability maturity (Humphrey, 1988) so that the US Department of Defense could assess defence contractors. Its five levels correspond to the use of good software engineering practice (bottom to top):

  1. Initial: essentially uncontrolled; process goals are satisfied and the scope of work is set out and communicated to the team.
  2. Repeatable (CMMI: Managed): product management procedures defined and used; documented project plans, resource management and process monitoring.
  3. Defined: process management procedures and strategies defined and used; standard organisational processes are adapted for each project.
  4. Managed (CMMI: Quantitatively managed): quality management strategies defined and used; statistical methods control sub-processes using measurements.
  5. Optimising: process improvement strategies defined and used; measurements and trends drive continuous improvement.

Limits: the model has too much overhead for small companies, and maturity estimation with agile processes is difficult, so it is used mainly by large companies.

SEI levels bottom to top: Initial, Repeatable, Defined, Managed, Optimising: "I Really Do Mean Order". The cycle: Measure → Analyse → Change.

Q7 Describe and explain the advantages of the following XP practices: (i) refactoring (ii) pair programming (iii) user stories.

5 marksCO1L1/L2Asked in CIE-1 2024Very likely
XP intro 0.5 + Each practice: meaning and advantages 1.5 × 3
Model answer

Extreme Programming (XP) is a very influential agile method that takes an "extreme" approach to iterative development: new versions may be built several times per day, increments are delivered to customers every 2 weeks, and all tests must be run for every build. Requirements are recorded as user stories, which drive each release:

(i) Refactoring

Refactoring is constant code improvement: the programming team look for possible improvements to the software and implement them immediately, even where there is no immediate need for them. XP argues that changes cannot be reliably anticipated, so instead of designing for change it keeps the code easy to change. Examples: re-organising a class hierarchy to remove duplicate code; tidying up and renaming attributes and methods; replacing inline code with calls to methods in a program library.

  • Improves understandability of the software, and so reduces the need for documentation.
  • Changes are easier to make, because the code is well-structured and clear.
  • Stops the structure degrading as increments are added, keeping the code simple and maintainable. (Limit: architecture refactoring is much more expensive.)

(ii) Pair programming

Programmers work in pairs, sitting together at the same computer, to develop the code. Pairs are created dynamically, so that all team members work with each other during development.

  • Common ownership of code: knowledge is spread across the team, which shares responsibility for problems.
  • Informal review process: each line of code is looked at by more than one person; cheaper and easier to organise than formal inspections.
  • Encourages refactoring, because the whole team benefits from improving the code.
  • Reduces project risk when team members leave. It is not necessarily inefficient: a pair can be as productive as two programmers working separately.

(iii) User stories

A user story is a scenario of use that might be experienced by a system user. The customer, who is part of the XP team, writes it on a story card with the developers. The team breaks each story into implementation tasks, which are the basis of schedule and cost estimates, and the customer chooses the stories for the next release by priority. Example: the Mentcare "Prescribing medication" story is split into tasks such as Change dose of prescribed drug, Formulary selection and Dose checking.

  • People relate to stories more easily than to a requirements document or use cases.
  • Stories get users involved in suggesting requirements.
  • Tasks give a direct basis for estimates and release planning; unimplemented stories can change or be discarded as requirements change. (Problem: it is hard to judge whether the stories are complete.)

Structure each part as meaning (2 lines) + 3 advantages. Name one refactoring example and the story-to-task idea; that is what examiners look for.

Q8 List the principles of agile methods and explain the Scrum sprint cycle with a neat labelled diagram.

5 marksCO1L2Asked in CIE-1 2024Very likely
Five principles 2 + Sprint cycle diagram 1.5 + Explanation 1.5
Model answer

Agile methods are incremental methods focused on rapid development, frequent releases and minimal documentation. The Agile Manifesto values individuals and interactions over processes and tools, working software over comprehensive documentation, customer collaboration over contract negotiation, and responding to change over following a plan. All agile methods share five principles:

PrincipleDescription
1. Customer involvementCustomers are closely involved throughout development; they provide and prioritise new requirements and evaluate the iterations.
2. Incremental deliveryThe software is developed in increments, with the customer specifying the requirements to be included in each increment.
3. People, not processThe skills of the team are recognised and exploited; members develop their own ways of working without prescriptive processes.
4. Embrace changeExpect the system requirements to change, and so design the system to accommodate these changes.
5. Maintain simplicityFocus on simplicity in the software and in the process; actively work to eliminate complexity.

Scrum is an agile method that focuses on managing iterative development. Its framework is centred on a set of sprints: fixed time periods (usually 2–4 weeks) in which a system increment is developed. A Scrum project has an outline planning phase, a series of sprint cycles, and a project closure phase.

  1. Product backlog: the starting point; the list of work to be done (features, requirements, user stories, engineering tasks), prioritised by the Product Owner.
  2. Review work and select items: the whole team works with the customer to select the features and functionality to be developed during the sprint.
  3. Plan sprint: the team estimates the work using its velocity and produces the sprint backlog; the team then organises itself to develop the software.
  4. Sprint: fixed length (2–4 weeks); the team is isolated, and all outside communication goes through the ScrumMaster, who protects it from distractions. A short daily Scrum meeting reviews progress.
  5. Review sprint: the work done is reviewed and presented to stakeholders; the output is potentially shippable software, and the next sprint cycle begins.

Agile principles: "Can I Pass Every Module?" = Customer involvement, Incremental delivery, People not process, Embrace change, Maintain simplicity.
