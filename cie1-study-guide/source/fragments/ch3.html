3.1 Rapid and agile development Must know

Rapid software development Must know

  • Rapid development and delivery is now often the most important requirement for software systems. Businesses may even accept lower quality or fewer features to get essential software deployed quickly.
  • Businesses operate in a fast-changing environment, so it is practically impossible to produce a set of stable software requirements. Customers cannot predict how a system will affect their work or which operations should be automated; the real requirements often become clear only after users have tried the system.
  • Software therefore has to evolve quickly to reflect changing business needs.
  • Plan-driven development (specify everything, then design, build and test) is essential for some types of system, e.g. safety-critical control systems, but it does not meet these business needs: each change forces rework, so software arrives long after it was specified.
  • Agile development methods (e.g. Extreme Programming, Scrum, DSDM) emerged in the late 1990s. Their aim was to radically reduce the delivery time for working software systems.

Ordering a tailor-made suit. Plan-driven: you give every measurement on day one and collect the suit months later; if your size or taste changed, alterations are expensive. Agile: the tailor gives you a fitting every two weeks and adjusts as you go.

What is agile development? Must know

Agile methods — incremental development methods that focus on rapid software development, frequent releases of the software, reducing process overheads by minimizing documentation, and producing high-quality code. Customer representatives are involved directly in development.

An increment is a small, working piece of new functionality added in each version; a stakeholder is anyone with an interest in the system (users, customers, managers, regulators). Agile development is characterized by:

  1. Interleaved specification, design and implementation. There is no detailed specification; design documentation is minimal or generated automatically.
  2. A series of versions (increments), with stakeholders involved in specifying and evaluating each version.
  3. Frequent delivery of new versions for evaluation, typically every 2–3 weeks.
  4. Extensive tool support, e.g. automated testing tools, configuration management and integration tools.
  5. Minimal documentation, focus on working code; informal communication instead of formal documents.

Agile

3.2 Agile methods Must know

The problem agile methods solved Important

In the 1980s and early 1990s, better software was thought to come from careful project planning, formalized quality assurance, tool-supported analysis and design methods, and rigorous controlled processes. This suited large, long-lived systems (aerospace, government) built by many teams. Applied to small and medium business systems, the overhead dominated: more time went on how to develop than on programming and testing, and every requirements change meant reworking the specification and design.

Dissatisfaction with these overheads led to agile methods, which:

  • focus on the code rather than the design;
  • are based on an iterative approach to software development;
  • are intended to deliver working software quickly and evolve it quickly to meet changing requirements.

Aim of agile methods: to reduce overheads in the software process (e.g. by limiting documentation) and to be able to respond quickly to changing requirements without excessive rework.

The Agile Manifesto Must know

Issued by the leading developers of agile methods (agilemanifesto.org). Learn it word for word:

“We are uncovering better ways of developing software by doing it and helping others do it. Through this work we have come to value:

  • Individuals and interactions over processes and tools
  • Working software over comprehensive documentation
  • Customer collaboration over contract negotiation
  • Responding to change over following a plan

That is, while there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more.”

In simple words: people talking to each other matter more than fixed procedures or tools; progress is measured by software that runs, not by documents; work with the customer rather than argue over a contract; adapt when requirements change instead of clinging to an old plan.

The manifesto does not call processes, documentation, contracts or plans useless; it values the left-hand items more. Writing "agile means no documentation" loses marks.

The principles of agile methods Must know

All agile methods share these principles, based on the manifesto (Sommerville Fig 3.2):

PrincipleDescription
Customer involvementCustomers should be closely involved throughout the development process. Their role is to provide and prioritize new system requirements and to evaluate the iterations of the system.
Incremental deliveryThe software is developed in increments, with the customer specifying the requirements to be included in each increment.
People, not processThe skills of the development team should be recognized and exploited. Team members should be left to develop their own ways of working without prescriptive processes.

"Can I Pass Every Module?" gives Customer involvement, Incremental delivery, People not process, Embrace change, Maintain simplicity.

Agile method applicability Important

  1. Product development: a software company developing a small or medium-sized product for sale. Virtually all software products and apps are now developed using an agile approach.
  2. Custom system development within an organization, where there is a clear commitment from the customer to be involved in development and few external rules and regulations affect the software.

They fit because the customer can talk to the team continuously, the software is stand-alone, and a small co-located team can communicate informally. A safety-critical, regulated system such as the textbook's insulin pump needs plan-driven analysis instead.

3.3 Agile techniques: Extreme Programming Must know

Extreme Programming (XP) — a very influential agile method, developed in the late 1990s, that introduced a range of agile techniques. It takes an "extreme" approach to iterative development: new versions may be built several times per day, increments are delivered to customers every 2 weeks, and all tests must be run for every build; the build is only accepted if the tests run successfully.

The XP release cycle Must know

In words: (1) the customer selects the user stories for this release by priority and time available; (2) developers break the stories into tasks; (3) tasks are estimated and the release is planned; (4) pairs develop, integrate and test (tests first, all tests must pass); (5) the software is released to the customer; (6) the customer evaluates the system, and new or changed stories start the next cycle.

Extreme programming practices Must know

All ten (Sommerville Fig 3.4). A unit test checks one small piece of code; integration means adding finished code into the whole system.

Principle or practiceDescription
1. Incremental planningRequirements are recorded on story cards, and the stories to be included in a release are determined by the time available and their relative priority. The developers break these stories into development "tasks" (see Figs 3.4 and 3.5).
2. Small releasesThe minimal useful set of functionality that provides business value is developed first. Releases of the system are frequent and incrementally add functionality to the first release.
3. Simple designEnough design is carried out to meet the current requirements and no more.
4. Test-first developmentAn automated unit test framework is used to write tests for a new piece of functionality before that functionality itself is implemented.
5. RefactoringAll developers are expected to refactor the code continuously as soon as potential code improvements are found. This keeps the code simple and maintainable.
6. Pair programmingDevelopers work in pairs, checking each other's work and providing the support to always do a good job.
7. Collective ownershipThe pairs of developers work on all areas of the system, so that no islands of expertise develop and all the developers take responsibility for all of the code. Anyone can change anything.
8. Continuous integrationAs soon as the work on a task is complete, it is integrated into the whole system. After any such integration, all the unit tests in the system must pass.
9. Sustainable paceLarge amounts of overtime are not considered acceptable, as the net effect is often to reduce code quality and medium-term productivity.
10. On-site customerA representative of the end-user of the system (the customer) should be available full time for the use of the XP team. In an XP process, the customer is a member of the development team and is responsible for bringing system requirements to the team for implementation.

Five groups. Plan: incremental planning, small releases, on-site customer. Design: simple design, refactoring. Code: pair programming, collective ownership, continuous integration. Test: test-first. People: sustainable pace. (3 + 2 + 3 + 1 + 1 = 10.)

Influential XP practices Important

XP has a technical focus and is not easy to integrate with management practice in most organizations. So although agile development uses XP practices, the method as originally defined is not widely used; companies pick the practices they want, often alongside Scrum (3.8). The key practices are: user stories for specification, refactoring, test-first development and pair programming (sections 3.4–3.7).

3.4 User stories for requirements Must know

Requirements always change, so agile methods have no separate requirements phase; they combine requirements elicitation (finding out what users need) with development.

User story — a scenario of use that might be experienced by a system user, written briefly on a story card by the customer and the team. The team aims to implement it in a future release.

  • In XP, a customer or user is part of the XP team and is responsible for making decisions on requirements.
  • User requirements are expressed as user stories or scenarios.
  • These are written on cards, and the development team breaks them down into implementation tasks. These tasks are the basis of schedule and cost estimates.
  • The customer chooses the stories for the next release based on their priorities and the schedule estimates (about two weeks of work). Unimplemented stories may change or be discarded as requirements change.

Example: the "prescribing medication" story Must know

From Mentcare, the textbook's patient information system for mental health care. A formulary is the approved list of drugs and their recommended doses. (The textbook tells the story about Kate, a doctor; the slides use "you".)

Strengths

  • People relate to stories much more easily than to a requirements document or use cases.
  • They get users involved in suggesting requirements.
  • Tasks give a direct basis for estimates and planning.

Problem: completeness

  • Hard to judge if enough stories cover all essential requirements.
  • Hard to judge if one story gives a true picture of an activity.
  • Experienced users leave things out when describing their work.

3.5 Refactoring Must know

Refactoring — constant code improvement: the programming team look for possible improvements to the software and implement them immediately, even where there is no immediate need for them. In plain words, tidying up code so it is clearer and better structured, without adding features.

Conventional wisdom: design for changeXP: refactoring
IdeaSpend time and effort anticipating changes, as this reduces costs later in the life cycleNot worthwhile: changes cannot be reliably anticipated (expected ones often never come)
PracticeAdd generality to the design up frontConstant code improvement, so changes are easy when they have to be implemented

Why refactor? Must know

In incremental development, local changes degrade the software structure (workarounds, duplicated code), making each further change harder. Refactoring prevents this:

  • The team looks for possible software improvements and makes them even where there is no immediate need.
  • It improves the understandability of the software and so reduces the need for documentation.
  • Changes are easier to make because the code is well-structured and clear.
  • However, some changes require architecture refactoring, and this is much more expensive. Delivery pressure can also delay refactoring.

Examples of refactoring Must know

  1. Re-organization of a class hierarchy to remove duplicate code (Fig 3.6).
  2. Tidying up and renaming attributes and methods to make them easier to understand.
  3. Replacement of inline code with calls to methods that have been included in a program library.

Development environments usually provide refactoring tools that find dependencies between code sections and make global changes.

3.6 Test-first development Must know

Incremental development has no full specification for an external test team to work from. So in XP, testing is central: the program is tested after every change, and development cannot proceed until all tests pass. XP testing features:

  1. Test-first development: tests are written before the code.
  2. Incremental test development from scenarios (user stories and their tasks).
  3. User involvement in test development and validation.
  4. Automated test harnesses run all component tests each time a new release is built.

Test-driven development (TDD) Must know

Test-first (test-driven) development — instead of writing code and then tests for it, you write the tests before the code, so they can be run while the code is written and problems are found during development.

  • Writing tests before code clarifies the requirements to be implemented. A test defines the interface and the expected behaviour, so ambiguities must be cleared up before coding.
  • Tests are written as programs rather than data so that they can be executed automatically. The test includes a check that it has executed correctly. It usually relies on a testing framework such as JUnit (for Java).
  • All previous and new tests are run automatically when new functionality is added, checking that it has not introduced errors.
  • Each task card generates one or more unit tests, and testing never falls behind coding ("test-lag" is avoided).

Example: test case for dose checking Important

This test checks Task 3 of Fig 3.5. It covers the wrong cases as well as the correct one, and would be written as a JUnit program before the dose-checking code.

Customer involvement

  • The customer helps develop acceptance tests (tests with customer data that check real needs are met) for the stories in the next release.
  • The customer in the team writes tests as development proceeds, so all new code is validated against what the customer needs.
  • But customers have limited time, cannot work full-time with the team, and may feel that giving the requirements was enough, so they may be reluctant to help with testing.

Test automation

  • Tests are written as executable components before the task is implemented.
  • They should be stand-alone, simulate the submission of input, and check the result meets the output specification.
  • An automated test framework (e.g. JUnit) makes it easy to write executable tests and submit a set of tests for execution.
  • There is always a set of tests that can be quickly run, so problems introduced by new code are caught immediately.

Problems with test-first development Must know

  1. Programmers prefer programming to testing and sometimes take short cuts, e.g. incomplete tests that do not check for all possible exceptions.
  2. Some tests are very difficult to write incrementally, e.g. unit tests for the "display logic" and workflow between screens of a complex user interface.
  3. It is difficult to judge the completeness of a set of tests. You may have many tests but not complete coverage, so crucial parts stay untested. A big test set can give a false impression that the system is correct.

3.7 Pair programming Must know

Pair programming — programmers work in pairs, sitting together at the same computer, developing the code together. Pairs are created dynamically, so that all team members work with each other during development.

Advantages

  1. Common ownership of code; knowledge spread across the team. The team as a whole owns the software and shares responsibility for its problems.
  2. Informal review process: each line of code is looked at by more than one person. This finds fewer errors than formal inspections but is cheaper and easier to organize.
  3. Encourages refactoring, because the whole team benefits from improving the system code.
  4. Reduces project risk: the knowledge sharing is very important because it reduces the overall risk to a project when team members leave.

Is it inefficient? Important

Pair programming is not necessarily inefficient: there is some evidence that a pair is more efficient than two programmers working separately. Results are mixed, though. With student volunteers (Williams et al., 2000), productivity was comparable to two individuals (fewer false starts and less rework); with experienced programmers (Arisholm et al., 2007), it showed a significant loss. The knowledge sharing alone may make it worthwhile.

3.8 Agile project management and Scrum Must know

Why agile project management? Important

The principal responsibility of software project managers is to manage the project so that the software is delivered on time and within the planned budget. The standard approach is plan-driven: managers draw up a plan showing what should be delivered, when, and who will work on the deliverables. Agile project management requires a different approach, adapted to incremental development and agile practices, which still gives managers visibility of progress.

Scrum Must know

Scrum — an agile method that focuses on managing iterative development rather than on specific agile practices. It provides a project management framework centred round a set of sprints, which are fixed time periods when a system increment is developed.

Scrum uses new terms (e.g. ScrumMaster, not project manager) and does not mandate practices such as pair programming, so it fits existing company practice; it is the most widely used agile method. Your slides give three phases:

  1. Outline planning phase: establish the general objectives for the project and design the software architecture.
  2. A series of sprint cycles, where each cycle develops an increment of the system.
  3. Project closure phase: wraps up the project, completes required documentation such as system help frames and user manuals, and assesses the lessons learned.

Scrum terminology Must know

Scrum termDefinition (Sommerville Fig 3.8)
Development teamA self-organizing group of software developers, which should be no more than 7 people. They are responsible for developing the software and other essential project documents.
Potentially shippable product incrementThe software increment that is delivered from a sprint. "Potentially shippable" means that it is in a finished state and no further work, such as testing, is needed to incorporate it into the final product. In practice, this is not always achievable.
Product backlogA list of "to do" items which the Scrum team must tackle. They may be feature definitions for the software, software requirements, user stories or descriptions of supplementary tasks that are needed, such as architecture definition or user documentation.
Product ownerAn individual (or possibly a small group) whose job is to identify product features or requirements, prioritize these for development and continuously review the product backlog to ensure that the project continues to meet critical business needs. The Product Owner can be a customer but might also be a product manager in a software company or other stakeholder representative.
ScrumA daily meeting of the Scrum team that reviews progress and prioritizes work to be done that day. Ideally, this should be a short face-to-face meeting that includes the whole team (the daily Scrum or stand-up).
ScrumMasterResponsible for ensuring that the Scrum process is followed and guides the team in the effective use of Scrum. He or she is responsible for interfacing with the rest of the company and for ensuring that the Scrum team is not diverted by outside interference. The Scrum developers are adamant that the ScrumMaster should not be thought of as a project manager. Others, however, may not always find it easy to see the difference.
SprintA development iteration. Sprints are usually 2–4 weeks long.
VelocityAn estimate of how much product backlog effort that a team can cover in a single sprint. Understanding a team's velocity helps them estimate what can be covered in a sprint and provides a basis for measuring improving performance.

Also used: the sprint backlog (the items selected for one sprint) and the Scrum board (a shared whiteboard with post-it notes showing the sprint backlog, work done, staff unavailability, etc.).

The Scrum sprint cycle Must know

  1. Product backlog (Fig 3.9): the starting point for planning; the list of work to be done on the project (features, requirements, user stories, engineering tasks).
  2. Review work and select items: the Product Owner prioritizes the backlog. In the selection phase the whole project team works with the customer to select the features and functionality to be developed during the sprint.
  3. Plan sprint: the team estimates the work using its velocity, producing the sprint backlog. Once these are agreed, the team organizes itself to develop the software.
  4. Sprint: fixed length, normally 2–4 weeks. The team is isolated from the customer and the organization; all communication goes through the Scrum master, whose role is to protect the team from external distractions. Daily Scrums track progress.
  5. Review sprint: at the end of the sprint the work done is reviewed and presented to stakeholders. The next sprint cycle then begins.

Sprints are never extended to finish late work; unfinished items go back to the product backlog.

Teamwork in Scrum Important

  • The Scrum master is a facilitator who arranges daily meetings, tracks the backlog of work to be done, records decisions, measures progress against the backlog and communicates with customers and management outside the team.
  • The whole team attends short daily meetings (Scrums) to share progress since the last meeting, problems that have arisen and plans for the following day, so everyone knows what is going on and can re-plan short-term work to cope with problems.

Scrum benefits Must know

  1. The product is broken down into a set of manageable and understandable chunks.
  2. Unstable requirements do not hold up progress.
  3. The whole team has visibility of everything, so team communication is improved.
  4. Customers see on-time delivery of increments and gain feedback on how the product works.
  5. Trust between customers and developers is established, and a positive culture is created in which everyone expects the project to succeed.

Benefits = C-U-V-F-T: Chunks, Unstable requirements no problem, Visibility, Feedback and on-time delivery, Trust ("Clever Users Value Fast Teams").

3.9 Scaling agile & agile vs plan-driven in practice Read once

Scaling agile methods Read once

Agile methods succeed for small and medium-sized projects built by a small co-located team (thanks, it is argued, to better communication). Scaling means coping with larger, longer projects with multiple teams, perhaps in different places. Scaling up: agile for large systems too big for a small team. Scaling out: agile across a large organization with long development experience. Keep the fundamentals: flexible planning, frequent releases, continuous integration, test-driven development, good communications.

Practical problems with agile methods Read once

ProblemDetail
ContractsAgile informality is incompatible with the legal approach to contracts. Specification-based contracts preclude interleaving, so contracts must pay for developer time, not functionality, which legal departments see as high risk.
MaintenanceAgile suits new development, yet most money goes on maintenance. Issues: are agile systems maintainable with minimal documentation, and can agile evolve a system for change requests? Key problems: lack of product documentation; keeping customers involved; development team continuity. Agile relies on the team knowing what has to be done, but in long-lifetime systems the original developers will not always be there.
Distributed teamsAgile methods are designed for small co-located teams, yet much development now uses worldwide distributed teams.

Agile vs plan-driven: which factors decide Important

Most projects include elements of both plan-driven and agile processes. The right balance depends on three groups of factors:

IssueQuestions to ask (plan-driven is favoured when…)
System issuesHow large? Too large for a small co-located team that communicates informally. What type? Needs a lot of analysis and a detailed design before implementation. Lifetime? Long-lifetime, so documentation must tell the support team the developers' intentions. External regulation? Regulated, so detailed documentation is needed for the safety case.
People and teamsHow skilled are the designers and programmers? Agile may need higher skill than translating a detailed design into code. How is the team organized? Distributed teams may need design documents. What support technology? Without design documents, IDE visualization and program-analysis tools are essential.
Organizational issuesTraditional engineering organizations have a plan-based culture. Is a detailed specification standard practice? Will customer representatives be available to give feedback on increments (is incremental delivery realistic)? Can informal agile fit a culture of detailed documentation?

Agile methods for large systems Read once

Six factors make large systems hard for agile (slide 60 draws them around "Large software system"):

  • System of systems: separate communicating systems, each built by its own team, often in different places and time zones.
  • Brownfield development: interaction with existing systems resists incremental development.
  • System configuration: much of the work is configuration, not original code.
  • Regulatory constraints: external rules limit how they are developed.
  • Prolonged procurement: long timescales make coherent teams hard to keep.
  • Diverse stakeholders: impossible to involve them all.

IBM's agility at scale model (slide 61; three nested circles). Core agile development: value-driven life-cycle, self-organizing teams, focus on construction. Disciplined agile delivery: risk+value driven life-cycle, self-organizing with appropriate governance framework, full delivery life-cycle. Agility at scale: disciplined agile delivery where scaling factors apply: large team size, geographic distribution, regulatory compliance, domain complexity, organization distribution, technical complexity, organizational complexity, enterprise discipline.

Scaling up to large systems

  1. A completely incremental approach to requirements engineering is impossible.
  2. There cannot be a single product owner or customer representative.
  3. It is not possible to focus only on the code of the system.
  4. Cross-team communication mechanisms have to be designed and used.
  5. Continuous integration is practically impossible; keep frequent builds and regular releases.

Multi-team Scrum

  1. Role replication: each team has a Product Owner for its work component, and a ScrumMaster.
  2. Product architects: one per team; together they design and evolve the overall architecture.
  3. Release alignment: release dates aligned so a demonstrable, complete system is produced.
  4. Scrum of Scrums: a daily meeting of team representatives to discuss progress and plan work.

Agile across organizations is hard because of (1) project managers reluctant to risk a new approach; (2) bureaucratic quality procedures and standards; (3) a wide range of skill levels (agile works best with high skills); (4) cultural resistance after long use of conventional processes.
