6.1 What is architectural design? Must know

Before builders lay a brick, an architect decides the overall layout of the building. Software needs the same big-picture plan. The software architecture of a system is its overall organization: the main parts (components or sub-systems) and the relationships between them. Architectural design produces this plan.

Architectural design — is concerned with understanding how a software system should be organized and designing the overall structure of that system.

Three points to write in any answer (slide 3):

  1. What it does — works out how the system should be organized and designs its overall structure.
  2. Where it sits — it is the first stage of the software design process and the critical link between design and requirements engineering, because it identifies the main structural components in a system and the relationships between them.
  3. What it produces — an architectural model that describes how the system is organized as a set of communicating components.
Component
A part of the system with one well-defined job (e.g. the "vision system" of a robot). Large components are called sub-systems.
Stakeholder
Anyone with an interest in the system: users, customers, managers, engineers.
Functional / non-functional
Functional requirements say what the system does; non-functional requirements say how well (performance, security, safety, availability, maintainability).

Requirements and architecture overlap Read once

Ideally a specification contains no design, but this is unrealistic except for very small systems. In practice requirements engineering and architectural design overlap: you may propose an abstract architecture that groups system functions or features into large-scale components or sub-systems, and use it to discuss the requirements with stakeholders.

Why architecture matters Must know

Software architecture affects the performance, robustness, distributability and maintainability of a system (Bosch). Individual components implement the functional requirements, but the dominant influence on the non-functional characteristics is the system's architecture. A study of "architecturally significant requirements" (Chen et al.) confirmed that non-functional requirements have the most significant effect on a system's architecture.

Agility and architecture Must know

Agile methods build software in small increments with little design done up front. Even so:

  • It is generally accepted that an early stage of agile processes is to design an overall system architecture. Incremental development of architectures is not usually successful.
  • Refactoring (restructuring software to improve it without changing what it does) individual components in response to change is relatively easy.
  • Refactoring the system architecture is usually expensive because it affects so many components: you may need to modify most system components to adapt them to the change.

Repainting a room is cheap; moving the load-bearing walls means rebuilding most of the house. Components are the rooms; the architecture is the load-bearing walls.

6.2 Example: a packing robot control system Must know

Sommerville's example is a packing robot that can pack different kinds of objects. It uses a vision component to pick out objects on a conveyor, identify the type of object and select the right kind of packaging. It then moves objects from the delivery conveyor to be packaged, and places packaged objects on another conveyor. Fig 6.2 is an abstract model: it shows only these components and the links between them.

How to read such a block diagram (also called a box-and-line diagram):

  • Each box represents a component.
  • Boxes within boxes mean the component has been decomposed into sub-components (Arm + Gripper controllers form one sub-system; Packaging selection + Packing form another).
  • Arrows mean that data and/or control signals pass from component to component in the direction of the arrow. When you redraw Fig 6.2, keep the arrow directions and the two shaded groups.

6.3 Architectural abstraction: small and large Important

Architectures can be designed at two levels of abstraction (amount of detail):

  • Architecture in the small is concerned with the architecture of individual programs. At this level, we are concerned with the way that an individual program is decomposed into components. Sommerville's Chapter 6 is mostly about this level.
  • Architecture in the large is concerned with the architecture of complex enterprise systems that include other systems, programs and program components. These enterprise systems may be distributed over different computers, which may be owned and managed by different companies.

6.4 Advantages of explicit architecture Must know

"Explicit" means the architecture is deliberately designed and written down, not left to emerge by accident. Bass, Clements and Kazman suggest that explicitly designing and documenting a software architecture has three advantages:

AdvantageWhat it means
1. Stakeholder communicationThe architecture is a high-level presentation of the system that may be used as a focus of discussion by a range of different system stakeholders.
2. System analysisMaking the architecture explicit at an early stage requires some analysis, so it becomes possible to analyse whether the system can meet its non-functional requirements. Architectural decisions have a profound effect on whether critical requirements such as performance, reliability and maintainability can be met.
3. Large-scale reuseAn architectural model is a compact, manageable description of how a system is organized and how its components interoperate. The architecture is often the same for systems with similar requirements, so it may be reusable across a range of systems. Product-line architectures may be developed: the same architecture is reused across a range of related systems.

Think CAR: Communication (with stakeholders), Analysis (of the system), Reuse (large-scale).

6.5 Architectural representations and their use Must know

Box-and-line (block) diagrams Must know

Simple, informal block diagrams showing entities and relationships (like Fig 6.2) are the most frequently used method for documenting software architectures. People from different disciplines can readily understand them. But they have been criticised (Bass et al.) because they lack semantics and are very abstract: they do not show the types (nature) of relationships between components, nor the externally visible properties of the entities or sub-systems. However, they are useful for communication with stakeholders and for project planning.

Semantics = precise meaning. In Fig 6.2, is the arrow from Vision system to Object identification a function call, a message or shared data? The diagram does not say. Nor does it show what services each component offers (its "externally visible properties").

Strengths

  • Simple, quick and informal to draw
  • Intuitive: domain experts and engineers can relate to them and join discussions
  • Not cluttered with detail; show the whole system
  • Managers find them helpful for project planning

Weaknesses

  • Lack semantics (no precise meaning)
  • Do not show the types of relationships between components
  • Do not show externally visible properties of sub-systems
  • Too abstract to be a complete design document

Whether this criticism matters depends on the use of architectural models: the requirements for model semantics depend on how the models are used.

Use of architectural models Must know

The apparent contradiction between theory ("block diagrams are poor") and industrial practice ("everyone uses them") arises because an architectural model is used in two ways:

1. As a way of facilitating discussion about the system design2. As a way of documenting an architecture that has been designed
AimA high-level architectural view, useful for communication with system stakeholders and project planning because it is not cluttered with detail.A complete system model that shows the different components in a system, their interfaces and their connections.
BenefitStakeholders can relate to it and understand an abstract view of the system, then discuss the system as a whole without being confused by detail. It identifies the key components, so managers can start assigning people to plan their development.A detailed description makes it easier to understand and evolve the system.
NotationInformal block diagrams are enough; for many projects they are the only architectural description.Ideally a more rigorous notation, e.g. an architectural description language (ADL, see 6.10).

A detailed, complete description leaves less scope for misunderstanding the relationships between components. However, it is expensive and time-consuming to develop, and it is practically impossible to know whether it is cost-effective, so this approach is not widely used.

6.6 Architectural design decisions Must know

Architectural design is a creative process in which you design a system organization that will satisfy the functional and non-functional requirements of a system. There is no formulaic (fixed, step-by-step) architectural design process: it differs depending on the type of system being developed, the background and experience of the system architect, and the specific requirements for the system. So it is best to think of architectural design as a series of decisions to be made rather than a sequence of activities.

However, a number of common decisions span all design processes, and these decisions affect the non-functional characteristics of the system. Based on their knowledge and experience, architects consider the fundamental questions in Fig 6.4.

Notes that turn the questions into an answer:

  • Q2 (distribution) — embedded systems and apps for PCs and mobile devices need not be distributed, but most large systems are distributed over many computers. The choice of distribution architecture is a key decision that affects performance and reliability.
  • Q4–Q6 (structure, decomposition, control) — Garlan and Shaw's idea of an architectural style covers these three: choose the most appropriate structure (e.g. client–server or layered); decide a strategy for decomposing components into sub-components; and, in control modelling, develop a general model of the control relationships between the parts, i.e. how the execution of components is controlled.
  • Q1 and Q3 are about reuse (6.7); Q7 is explained in 6.8; Q8 (views and notations) in 6.9–6.10.
  • Evaluating the design is difficult: the true test is how well the system meets its requirements when in use, but you can compare it with reference architectures or generic patterns.

6.7 Architecture reuse Important

Each system is unique, but architectures are often reused. When designing, you decide what your system has in common with broader classes of applications and how much knowledge from those architectures you can reuse. Three forms:

Same domain

Systems in the same application domain often have similar architectures that reflect domain concepts.

Product lines

Application product lines are built around a core architecture with variants that satisfy particular customer requirements.

Patterns or styles

The architecture may be designed around one or more architectural patterns or "styles", which capture the essence of an architecture and can be instantiated in different ways.

Architectural pattern (style) — a description of a system organization, such as a client–server organization or a layered architecture. It captures the essence of an architecture that has been used in different software systems. ("Pattern" and "style" now mean the same thing.)

6.8 Architecture and system characteristics Must know

Because non-functional characteristics depend so closely on the architecture, the choice of architectural style and structure should depend on the non-functional requirements. For each critical requirement there is a matching architectural strategy:

If this is critical……use this architecture (slide wording)How and why (textbook detail)
PerformanceLocalise critical operations and minimise communications. Use large rather than fine-grain components.Keep critical operations in a few components on the same computer, not across the network. Large components reduce communications, as most interactions happen inside a component. Replicating the system on different processors may also help.
SecurityUse a layered architecture with critical assets in the inner layers.The most critical assets are protected in the innermost layers, with a high level of security validation applied to these layers.
SafetyLocalise safety-critical features in a small number of sub-systems.Co-locating safety-related operations reduces the cost of safety validation and may allow a protection system that safely shuts the system down on failure.
AvailabilityInclude redundant components and mechanisms for fault tolerance.Spare (redundant) components make it possible to replace and update components without stopping the system. Fault tolerance = continuing to work when a part fails.
MaintainabilityUse fine-grain, replaceable components.Small, self-contained components can readily be changed. Separate producers of data from consumers; avoid shared data structures.

Conflicts and trade-offs Must know

There is potential conflict between some of these architectures, so a compromise must be found:

Requirements in conflictWhy they pull apartCompromise
Performance vs maintainability (textbook example)Performance wants a few large components; maintainability wants many small, fine-grain components.Use different architectural patterns or styles for separate parts of the system.
Security vs the rest (textbook point)Security is now almost always a critical requirement, so it cannot simply be traded away.Design an architecture that maintains security while also satisfying the other non-functional requirements.
Security vs performance (follows from the strategies)Layers with security validation add extra steps between components; performance wants communication minimised.Keep time-critical operations together within one layer; apply the heaviest checks only to the inner layers.

In Mentcare (the clinics' patient information system), patient records are the critical asset, so they would sit in the innermost layer. In the insulin pump, the safety-critical dose computation and checks would be co-located in one small component that can be validated thoroughly.

6.9 Architectural views Must know

Whether a model is used to focus discussion or to document a design, two questions arise (slide 17):

  1. What views or perspectives are useful when designing and documenting a system's architecture?
  2. What notations should be used for describing architectural models? (see 6.10)

No single diagram can show everything: each architectural model only shows one view or perspective of the system. It might show how a system is decomposed into modules, how the run-time processes interact, or the different ways in which components are distributed across a network. All of these are useful at different times, so for both design and documentation you usually need to present multiple views of the software architecture, just as a house needs a floor plan, a wiring plan and a plumbing plan.

Krutchen's 4+1 view model Must know

In his well-known 4+1 view model of software architecture, Krutchen suggests four fundamental architectural views, linked through common use cases or scenarios (the "+1"). A use case or scenario describes one typical interaction with the system, e.g. "a doctor records a consultation".

ViewWhat it shows (exam wording)Useful forExample: Mentcare (illustration)
LogicalThe key abstractions in the system as objects or object classes. It should be possible to relate the system requirements to entities in this view.Relating requirements to the designClasses such as Patient, Consultation, Prescription, Clinic, Staff member.
ProcessHow, at run-time, the system is composed of interacting processes.Judging performance, availability and other non-functional characteristicsA client process on each clinic PC talks to a server process that handles patient-record requests.

Process view (run-time processes) ≠ development view (code split among teams); physical view (hardware) ≠ logical view (classes). The "+1" links the four views; it is not a fifth view.

The conceptual view, and how many views are needed Important

Hofmeister et al. suggest similar views but add a conceptual view: an abstract view of the system that can (1) be the basis for decomposing high-level requirements into more detailed specifications, (2) help engineers make decisions about components that can be reused, and (3) represent a product line rather than a single system. The packing robot architecture (Fig 6.2) is an example of a conceptual view.

In practice, conceptual views are almost always developed during design, to explain the architecture to stakeholders and inform decision making. Other views may be developed as different aspects are discussed, but it is rarely necessary to develop a complete description from all perspectives. Architectural patterns can also be associated with the different views.

6.10 Representing architectural views Important

Which notation should describe the views: the UML (Unified Modeling Language, the standard diagrams for object-oriented software) or ADLs (architectural description languages)?

  • Some people argue that the UML is an appropriate notation for describing and documenting system architectures.
  • Sommerville disagrees (he is the "I" on slide 20): the UML does not include abstractions appropriate for high-level system description; it was designed for object-oriented systems, and object classes are too close to the implementation. He prefers informal notations, quicker to write and easy to draw on a whiteboard. The UML is most valuable for documenting an architecture in detail or for model-driven development (where models are used to generate the system).
  • ADLs have been developed but are not widely used. Their basic elements are components and connectors, with rules for well-formed architectures. They are precise, but specialist languages that domain experts find hard to use.

Informal models and notations, such as the UML, will remain the most common. Agile users say detailed design documents are mostly unused; Sommerville largely agrees. Except for critical systems, do not develop a detailed description from all four of Krutchen's views: develop the views useful for communication and do not worry about completeness.
